Hi everyone,

Let me tell you about my brave foray into the post office here. Post offices are deadly places to do any business because there are choices involved, and usually a queue behind you, and a public servant who is more than likely stuck in a poorly paid unfulfilling job.

The PO here, or PTT, was an even more depressing environment than the ones we are used to in the wide brown land. Pale grey walls, harsh LED lighting and complete lack of decoration, apart from a portrait of Ataturk gazing benignly into the distance. The big glass windows could have done with a wash, and the atmosphere felt a bit like an interrogation room in an episode of Border Protection on TV. Come to think of it, not all that different from those in Oz.

I had a small parcel to send to some friends, so I thought it would be easily done...hand over the parcel, smile a lot, pay for the stamp and that would be it.

Step one was getting the place in the queue, ie take a ticket from the machine with the touch screen.

Problem one, there were three choices, all in Turkish, none of which I could decipher. At the PO you can pay bills, get insurance, pick up parcels....much the same as in Oz, so going in the right queue seemed important. So I made a frantic jab at the screen, and out came a ticket which didn’t seem to correspond to any of the windows.

Eventually I made it to one of them, and smiled, said hello and handed the parcel over. The guy checked it, then indicated that I needed a sender’s address on the back. I’m travelling I replied. Doesn’t matter came the reply...something like ‘computer says no’. So I returned home to get the address of the hotel I’ll briefly be in in Istanbul next week. Back to the PO.

A different queue this time, the same smile.

Then a rapid question....I caught the last word, ‘normal’, which handily is the same as English, so I gathered that there was a choice presented...fast or slow. Grasping at a straw I said ‘normal’ as though I actually wanted it. Hmmm. She wasn’t convinced.

What was in the parcel she asked ( I’m ok with short sentences)...I told her, and she opined ( and I’m guessing here) that normal would be too slow (2 weeks!) and not very safe. Ok, I replied, let’s go with fast. She indicated the stand behind me, which I could vaguely see through the rapidly lengthening queue, and indicated that I should get a form from there. She helped me fill it in, putting the paragraph-sized address willy-nilly over minuscule spaces that said postcode, ID number, weight, email, mother’s shoe size. That done she transcribed it all into the computer to generate the sticker for the plastic bag that my parcel went into. She did ask me my phone number, twice, but I declined: well  actually I lied by saying that I didn’t have one. I could just imagine the Kafkaesque conversation in Turkish, from  a P.O. employee telling me that...well whatever. I preferred not to know.

I paid the small amount, said thank you, smiled and fought my way through the throng that had built up in the 15 minutes of my negotiation. Gasp.

When I was here 12 years ago I lost my credit card. I got a replacement quickly. This replacement  however had no pin, so it wouldn’t work in the ATMs. I had to go into the bank and negotiate a cash advance, which was often a whole morning’s entertainment. Compared with that ordeal today’s effort was child’s play.

On Monday night Murat and I went to a concert in the theatre around the corner, where a trio of guitarists and a bandoneon worked their way through a series of tangos. The bandoneon is like a button accordion, and is really characteristic of this music. It was a good night, even though I was somewhat tangoed out by the end.

A boy’s circumcision here is a big event. It happens around 7-9 years old, and the kid is made a huge fuss of...presents, family meals, recognition of passing an milestone....and some pretty fancy prince-like costumes. In the bazaar here there are rows of shops selling the gear. Often the dummy in the window is carrying a big sword as part of the regalia. I guess the symbolism is lost on most of them.

The veg and fruit in the markets seem really cheap, and even at this time of the year there are luscious tomatoes to be had, these ones for a bit over a dollar a kilo.

*(photo missing: 78AD9FDB-9A44-453C-A741-7DAEDAF19B75-1024x768.jpeg)*

Maral did warn me though that at this time of the year such fruit is likely to be full of added hormones, so if I start to grow purple manboobs, then lay off them for a while.

I opined to Murat that food in the markets seemed very cheap (and it is for a cashed-up foreigner like me), but he put it into perspective from the point of view of a local. He, and say a school teacher, would get about $1000 a month in wages...remember that he is an electronic engineer working in the airforce.  So we as school teachers in Oz would be getting 6 or 7 times the wage that they get. So for them, a kilo of tomatoes costs the equivalent of 7 or 8 dollars!!! That was a bit of a reality check.

I had dinner and a good chat with another friend on Tuesday night. She, Hanife, is a lecturer at a uni here, and her boy is Mustafa. They are both good to be with.  I met them through Glenn Rowles, whom some of you may remember from his time cutting a swathe through Kerang 30 years ago. He was teaching here two years ago, but now is in China.

I have a friend there that I first met 12 years ago in a village in central Turkey ( I’ll relate that story sometime soon), and we’ll catch up for a drink and a reminisce while I’m there.
