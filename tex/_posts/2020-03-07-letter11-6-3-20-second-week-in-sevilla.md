Hi again.

The life here has settled into a routine, one might have said a rut. The classes now start at 9am, which is a bit of a shock to the system as it involves my getting up in the dark (who does that?)...the sun gets up at 7.50, so if it's good enough for the sun it should be good enough for me. However, the street lights outside, once you open the blinds, give a semblance of the dawn, so it’s ablutions and breakfast, and off to walk to the school which takes about 40 mins.

The new teacher is a 40 yo and has the energy of an aerobics instructress, and her classes are fashioned along the same lines as well. She speaks louder and more distinctly than the other teacher, so I can hear, and have a better chance of understanding. I am speaking much more confidently now, and thanks to the online courses I have been doing, I have a decent handle on the grammar that we do so that I retain the finer points.

After the 2 hr class, I walk back, maybe buying some food on the way, do the homework, write a story, have some lunch and then fall in a bit of a concentration black hole. After a nap, sorry, siesta, I do a bit of wandering around the city, have dinner at the house around 7. The sun goes to bed at half past, and not all that many hours later so do I.

In this district, Triana, there is a statue commemorating the life of Spain’s arguably greatest bull fighter. Here’s his statue, just next to the bridge named after Isabella II..

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/5F0AEB97-CB0C-4BF5-8C12-334DB9548349.jpeg' | relative_url }})

His name is Juan Belmonte, and he lived from 1892 to 1962. He grew up in Triana, so that’s why he is commemorated here. He had slightly deformed legs, and his style of bull-fighting was markedly different from others: he stood in the one place, let the bull run very close, rather than dancing away, which was the usual style. He was gored several times (not surprisingly) once where he was a pinned to the wall by the bull’s horns. Maybe that’s why there’s a big hole through the statue, or maybe it’s a nod towards the British sculptor Henry Moore. He did eventually retire and became a gentleman farmer, raising bulls to be slaughtered in the bull ring.

If you look through the hole in the statue in the right direction, (which I did)....

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/262AD2F8-D246-48D7-AC35-FC2D5CD530C2.jpeg' | relative_url }})

...you can see the Giralda. That’s the bell tower of the cathedral, and is a symbol of the city. It was actually the minaret of a mosque, but when the Catholic Royals (Ferdinand and Isabella) arrived in the 15th century, they dismantled the mosque, kept the minaret, made it taller and called it a bell tower.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/C059DED1-C19F-48FC-A75A-D2450DAF9E0B.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The church they had built in the mosque’s place is the biggest gothic cathedral in the known universe, and the third biggest church anywhere. Inside it is humongous.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/17F1FEAA-7CB3-4202-933F-246902A64262.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Outside the cathedral are the usual statues of saints to provide inspiration to those who need it. They are generally martyrs, and are identifiable by the instrument of their martyrdom.

This one is Simon the Zealot, (although the internet tells me that ‘zealot’ is probably a mistranslation of the original Greek, and it should know) one of the shadowy disciples who we don't have much of a bio for in the gospels. .. come to think of it, do we have many facts about them at all? They invented a back story though, which ended in his martyrdom, by being sawn in half ...lengthwise. He is carrying the crosscut saw. Cripes, what a way to go!

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/013E19D3-B344-4170-8518-3F4E16BBC24B.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The next is the third pope and saint, St Clement. He was too good at converting people, so the Romans had him tossed into the sea with an anchor around his neck, according to the stories.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/6E0AFD99-A086-4F43-BE1D-84A450BB3962.jpeg' | relative_url }})

And the last one for today is St Andrew. I was going to suggest that he was an early maths teacher, but the story goes that he was the first of Jesus’s disciples, the brother of Peter, and later mythologising had him crucified, but on a multiplication sign, rather than plus. He got adopted as the patron saint of Scotland (along with many other jobs, including sore throats), and his cross is the white one in the Union Jack...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/12C7F5F6-3CB0-4AAA-B967-93602B210320.jpeg' | relative_url }})

There is a pastie-like treat, Argentinian in origin, which is popular here, called an empanada. Here’s a pic of part of the display in one shop...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/E1A91BD7-658B-4C9A-9263-132A5BDFE910.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Here’s part of the menu on the wall...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/37CFCA25-B779-4521-B47A-5AD2914F52DC.jpeg' | relative_url }})

I had the Cebolla y Queso one which was delicious. Bakers in Australia could well get gourmeted up and do the same...the Aussie pastie is such a glugfest of boringness in comparison.

There is one tram line here that runs through the middle of the old area. The trams are very modern and swish, but there are no overhead wires!

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/0053804F-DF96-4A88-8B4C-EEB83C99D829.jpeg' | relative_url }})

When the tram glides into the tram stop, its pantograph expands onto the overhead structure, whence it gains more than enough energy to get to the next stop. Seems like a great system to me, but only where there is a space dedicated to the tramline. I can imagine that there would be problems if one such tram got stuck in a traffic jam in Melbourne and ran out of juice.

I had a few minutes of quiet time in a splendid little Gothic church only about 4 Hail Marys from the monster cathedral. It’s quite tiny, but with a great piece behind the altar...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/03/FE63A39D-A273-43DB-B2C0-E9744AF0A524.jpeg' | relative_url }})

In the churches here there’s is often minimal reference to the big J. This one is typical, with lots of statues and pictures representing Mary, and Jesus only getting a guernsey as a baby. Spanish men all love their mothers (my teacher from last week noted that at film awards the winners always thank their mothers first) so giving prominence to a mother who will intercede with the unintercedewithable father would make sense. Only slightly Freudian ....

One afternoon when I was slouching along the main drag between my place and the Puente Isabella II, I came across this group of women doing a sort of progressive sensuality dance. It’s called the Sevilliana. It was quite splendid to watch...
