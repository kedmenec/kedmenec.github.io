Hi all.

With any luck you had the choice to read, or to ignore, the last letter. Here’s the next one.

The weather here is warming up nicely...22 and clear, with an occasional thunderstorm to settle the dust. The word for thunder in Turkish is ‘gök gürültüsü’ which translates as ‘sky’s noise’. A bit more descriptive than the English.

Classes move on apace, and development is proceeding. Still, there remains the false impression of capability: in the class, with no distraction, a teacher who speaks clearly with standard Turkish, and the escape hatch of reverting to English when the going gets tough, one can easily fool oneself into thinking that all will be less terrifying in the outside world. It isn’t. Once the rabbit-in-the-headlights response starts, in the crowded restaurant, in the face of a machine gun enthusiasm, the scales are ripped from the eyes. Still, enforced humility and a reality check are not a bad thing, and the increasingly frequent successes are balms to the wounded ego.

On Saturday I bussed to Bergama, a couple of hours north of here. It is the modern city nestling up to the hill where the ancient city of Pergamom was, and bits of which remain.

I’ve been to the archaeological site before, so gave that a miss, but couldn’t avoid, and didn’t want to avoid, seeing it from almost anywhere in the city below.

The old city was massive, with temples to various gods, a huge library, rivalling that of Alexandria, and which Mark Antony gave to his squeeze du jour Cleopatra as a gift. Also the Asklepion, a healing centre, where in the second century a genius named Galen worked, although it had been goıng for several centurıes before he arrıved. He did detailed studies on anatomy and the theory of sickness, or perhaps we should say unwell ness. His cures involved mud baths, diet modification, music therapy, massage etc, restoring the body to health so that it could fight the infection. It seems to be a better solution to problems than the modern paradigm of dealing- with- the-symptoms-rather-than-the-causes practices. Although it probably doesn’t work with a broken leg.

When a version of Christianity became the state religion in the 4th century, the Asklepion was closed down (the church wanted sole control of this and the other world view I guess) along with all other heterodox religious institutions (including other I Christian manifestations) but Galen’s writings survived because they were translated into Arabic, and used in middle eastern medicine, while Europe descended into purging and prayer and self flagellation as a response to unwellnessness. Only after the Crusades and the Arab invasion of Spain did his ideas get back into Europe, where they were the foundation of medical learning until the 18 or 19th century.

The origin of the twin snakes as a symbol of medicine is claimed for Galen. This poster in the museum explains all...it’s pretty fuzzy. Maybe you could consult Wikipedia if you want to know more.

The city below isn’t up to all that much for the languid tourist, and the old and jolly carpet seller that I spent quite a while chatting with confirmed that, languid or otherwise, tourists have been very thin on the ground in recent years.

The museum had the usual headless, noseless and other appendageless marble statues, some very old carpets and kilims and a cool and serene atmosphere.

There is another ruin in the town which, 2000 years ago was a monster religious site dedicated to the cult of the Egyptian goddess Serapis. The site was about 200x100m, but much of it has been covered by the city. Still, some impressive walls remain, and  are being stabilised by UNESCO funds, and jackhammering workers. Once Christianity had become the flavour du jour in the 4 century, the place was decommissioned, and a church constructed inside. In that crazy book Revelation, the church in Bergama (aka Pergamom), along with that at Ephesus and Izmir (aka Smyrna) are counted among the seven churches of Asia...all of them in western Turkey.  A millennium or so later, when the god of choice changed again, a mosque joined the scene.

I got a  taxi to the bus station when it was time to leave and chatted with the driver, in Turkish, quite happily, and my comment that there seemed to be a lot of mosques in the town (the simultaneous calls to prayer at lunchtime were something to hear) prompted him to unload extensively on religiosity (in this place there are too many mosques and not many muslims), on the president and his push to establish schools where a significant proportion of the curriculum is religious, and then the state of politics in general. It was most enlightening.

I got the taxi to the bus station, located kilometres out of the town, because I assumed that that was where you got the bus from. But when I got there there was only one of the bus company ticket offices manned, and no one else around...no travellers. When the bus arrived it was almost full. What was the secret to this enigma? I reasoned that you could actually get on the bus at its office in the town (finding it may have been another task, but not insurmountable), thus saving the taxi ride. It all makes sense when you strip away the preconceptions.

On Sunday I went for a stroll around the bazaar here in Izmir, hoping to find a yapışkan satıcı (that’s the sticky salesman, in case you forgot) to talk with, and found one, or rather he found me. He is a 38 yo type, even though he could easily have been taken for 25, has two late teenage kids and was good fun to talk to. His English was pretty good, all of it learned on the street and through watching Australian soap operas on TV. We drank tea, then turned to the display case of watches...good quality fakes...and I tried a few on, then declined. No matter...we chatted on, he showed me some leather jackets, which I tried on then declined. No matter. He did get onto religion, No matter, but we agreed that what was important in life was the quality of the way you treat other people, and that when you cark it and you come before god (I didn’t agree with this detail, but the seen as a metaphor I could run with it) it wouldn’t matter what religion, if any, you were, as that is largely a matter of what society you are born into, but which of the two doors you went through would be determined by how you lived your life. I have found this in other discussions with muslims too...it is a stark contrast to exclusivist religions like Christianity that insist that they are the only one, and that all the others are wrong, and that their adherents are of necessity bound for the hot place. Of course in Muslim conquests there are forced conversions, just as with other religions, but that is a political thing, just as Christians were persecuted in Roman times not because they were Christians, but because they refused to offer sacrifices to the prevailing gods, thus endangering the viability of the nation or of society.

Sunday saw the celebration of Victory Day, a very important commemoration for the Turkish nation. On this day in 1915, the sick man of Europe, the decaying Ottoman Empire was being attacked by the might of the Allied Forces, but sank several of the battle ships, and later at Gallipoli repelled the land based attempt at invasion. Everywhere huge Turkish flags are hung, and speeches and gatherings and celebrations are being held. Here is are pics of the clock tower in Konak, with associated flags.

Two more weeks of school to go then a few weeks of travelling. It all goes so quickly.

One last pic...there is a pastry thing, filo pastry with cheese, spinach and dill in it, a bit like spanakopita. In Turkish it’s called börek or maybe gözleme. There are shops that just make and sell it, but in my experience this was the best. In the markets women sell the pastry by the sheet and I suppose you put your own filling in, then fry in a pan until it is hot and delicious. Maral did it for breakfast.

All the best for the next week
