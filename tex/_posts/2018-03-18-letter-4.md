This is the first time I’ve ever blogged, so maybe you aren’t actually reading this, and I, St Francis- like, am preaching to the creatures that only exist in cyberspace. Scary thought!

Last weekend, on the Sunday, I trained and bussed to a seaside town called Foça (pronounced to rhyme with gotcha).  It’s a sleepy little fishing place, or it was; now it is a sleepy little tourist place except on the weekends when it is rudely awakened by the hordes of visitors. The little harbour is ringed ( or is that rung?) by little fishing boats in the water, and restaurants on the land. Here’s a pic or two...

There were quite a few people wandering and enjoying the sun. Also groups of young men, whose hair was cut very short and who seemed mostly on their telephones. It turns out that there is an army base nearby, and these guys would be national servicemen, having their one day a week off the base, and able to use telephones. Occasionally there was a solitary guy with his phone to the ear...maybe a friendless fellow from a distant province, dying of homesickness, caught in the web of proving he was able to take it, but shriveling on the inside. Maybe not, and I am just overindulging in empathy. Turkish men have to do between 6 and 14 months, depending on their education, or so I believe, so for a village lad from the east who has only really known village life it’s a bit of a change.

Turkish has a suffix that goes on verbs and other things, that in many cases indicates that the information hasn’t been experienced first hand, but only ...er...second hand. It gives a level of subtlety that we have to use ‘so I’m told, I heard, they say...’ or for an inference.  It would have been useful in the paragraph above.

In Izmir, and elsewhere, there is a policy of dealing with street dogs, where they are caught, neutered, vaccinated and tagged, then let back on the street. The upshot is that there are lots of big lazy dogs, sleeping on the footpath. But there are no little dogs, for obvious reasons, and eventually, I suppose, the dog population will dwindle to...very few. My teacher told me that the flat-dwelling types who get the cute puppy for the kids, then find that it grows into some huge, freedom restricting hound, abandon them in the street...wait, doesn’t that happen in the wide brown land? The same neutering thing doesn’t apparently operate with cats though, and people leave dried food and water out in the street for them. At night there are lots of them foraging in the dumpster bins. I suppose they keep the vermin population down a bit.

I’ve  been back to the Orchide Cafe, the favourite one near the Hisar mosque, a few times in the last week, and get the full welcoming trick each time. I’m usually there at lunchtime, strange that!, and Murat the waiter importuning passers by to sit is amazing to watch. It is wall to wall restaurants here, and there is rivalry with the one directly opposite, so the young guy from there and the guys from my(!) place have no time to rest (well, just enough to light another fag and check the phone) in the relentless quest for customers. At times they both descend on some potentials, like piranhas in a fish tank.

These touts are very common in high turnover places where the competition is great, and for we foreigners, there is always the wish to explain why we are not wanting to eat or buy a carpet...not wanting to hurt the guy’s feelings. It seems really rude just to power by without making eye contact, and they understand that, and exploit it. The ‘hey sir, where are you from?’, as an overture is really hard to resist, especially if you are in the country to chat and get to know the place, rather than to look at the monuments, then go back to the hotel or cruise ship.

So how to deal with the approach/avoidance conflict of not wanting to be brusque, but wanting some choice in who you talk to?

I’ve found a way that works for me. The typical body language for refusal here is the tilt the head back slightly, raise the eye brows, don’t smile but do maintain eye contact, click your tongue while pursing the lips Trumpesquely and to touch your chest vaguely in the heart area. It all sounds a bit rude (and a bit like chewing gum and walking at the same time) but it’s ok here, and it works, especially when followed with a smile and a touch to the shoulder of the importuner.

The Turkish word for a tout is ‘yapişkan satıcı’ which translates as ‘a sticky salesman’.
