Hi all,

This is the last letter about Tunis.

I flew back to Istanbul on the 27th Feb. Ten days in Tunis was probably a bit excessive, and after a week I found myself wringing the internet for things to do, but just wandering and seeing where you end up has its charms.

On the last Saturday I got the train south to the resort town of Sousse, whose claim to infamy is the awful terrorist attack that took place on the beach a few months after the one in the Bardo museum in 2015.

The town is very orderly, and the huge medina is surrounded by massive golden-coloured crusader- proof walls. Inside it is a labyrinth of winding, cobbled ways, lots of shops with occasionally predatory salesmen, and buckets of atmosphere. Since the attack the tourist industry has shrivelled markedly, and the salesmen I chatted with said it is only gradually getting back into gear.

The museum was a nice little one with lots of mosaics from Roman times. This total immersion font is from around the 5th century I think, when the Roman Empire (by this time the main conduit for the spread of Christianity) centred on Constantinople, held sway across Northern Africa.

The train trip cost $5 and lasted a bit over 2hours, clacking its way through massive orchards of olive trees, past huge aloes (very welcoming) and copses of prickly pear.

It was a pleasant way to spend a day.

I have continued to dine at the next door restaurant, and feel like a local there now. When I enter, after shaking hands with as many as will, I ask ‘what’s on the menu tonight?’ There is a reply, which I might or mightn’t understand, I nod ok, then find a seat. Up (the major seating area is at the top of a spiral staircase) comes the half bottle of white, some spicy dips, and later, the meal. One night it was little octopuses, really tasty, another, tuna cross sections (well, I imagine that they were a little vexed anyway). They look burnt in the pic, but a decent dose of browning is needed to avoid the fish being mistaken for tofu!

![]({{ '/assets/uploads/2019/02/DFD190F1-D86D-452D-AB25-3DAD51ECDD67.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The three yellow bits are actually potato, not banana.

I do go further afield for lunch, so I’m not as bound by habit as it appears.

I got enveigled into a carpet shop last Sunday, and, after eventually telling the bloke that I wasn’t interested in what he was showing me, and that I had a considerable number of century old Turkish kilims on my walls, and that he’d better show me something interesting or I would finish my mint tea and leave (well...the last bits were implied, but I didn’t find him very simpatico, so I didn’t feel like wasting his time or mine), he pulled out some very old Berber made kilims, none of which appealed, but at least they were something to talk about. I did ask him how life had changed since the revolution of 2011, when the dictator in charge was deposed. He replied that life was a lot harder now because the cruise ships weren’t coming (which he attributed to the revolution) and that democracy is all very well, but he can’t feed it to his kids! He added that before, they knew who the corrupt people were (the president and his wife and her family) but now they didn’t know who they were! We in Oz are rather lucky just to have Matthias Corman not checking his credit card or Georgina Downer passing off a government grant as something that she, as a failed candidate, had something to do with.

Also on Sunday...the weather had turned pretty foul, so I was just wandering...I lobbed into the big Cathedral of St Vincent de Paul that sits on the main drag. There are some pics in the first letter. There was a mass going on, the air was heavy with incense and the place was pretty full. Who were these types? I wondered. A stealthy stroll up the side aisle led me to see that they were black Africans, rather than Arabic Tunisians. Hmmm.

![]({{ '/assets/uploads/2019/02/05894628-76BB-4C16-A9FE-E2F0BB83E6CE.jpeg' | relative_url }})

There’s a whopper organ in the loft, and a small choir of strong singers whose chanting was amplified through the place. The singing, architecture, smells, bells and costumed players around the altar really generated an emotional response. I find it a bit more appealing and authentic than glitzy happy-clappy music and glossolailing, but each to his own.

The form of Arabic that is spoken here doesn’t sound much like the guttural hawking the Arabs further east use (it makes me wonder whether there is any other reason that one of the states is called Qatar!). Here they seem to have a lot of French included, and the books tell me that there’s Italian and Spanish in the mix too. Whatever the recipe, when two or three are gathered together, the tone of voice nearly always sounds as if WW3 is about to break out, and in the restaurants, when there are tables of liquored up locals, even with the smoke-induced acoustic attenuation, it can sound like the dressing rooms when the footy team has won the granny.

I got into the courtyard at the front of the big mosque on Monday afternoon. It’s a very old structure, built in the 8th century, and the columns that hold the cloisters up are reputed to have come from the site of ancient Carthage.

The prayer hall, the business part of the mosque, is only for muslims, so I didn’t go in. I did wonder how they could tell if you were or not though. And what would ‘they’ do if they sprang you? I didn’t care to find out...when in Rome, do as etc.

There is an interesting aspect to the currency here. You aren’t allowed to take Tunisian notes or coins in or out of the place. If you end up with any when you are leaving you can exchange them at the airport, for euros or usd, but you have to have the ticket from the atm, receipting (sorry, that sounds a bit of neologising...oops, there’s another) the original exchange. A trap for the feckless traveller.

Connected with money...in the past when I have travelled os I’ve used my Bendigo Bank debit card, which has attracted a 3% currency conversion fee and a $5 foreign atm fee on each withdrawal. Pretty excessive but I just put up with it. But this time I’ve been using a Citibank Plus debit card (dead easy to open the account online) which has no fees at all and a decent exchange rate. You can use travel cards from the banks, where you load up with usd or euros or other major currencies, but this Citibank strikes me as being very easy and more flexible. Disclaimer...I am not a paid spruiker for Citibank!

I went to the same barber for the third time the day before I left. The first time, soon after I had arrived in the town, he had grunted a bit, did a fairly perfunctory lathering, but the shave, after the pain of what seemed like being scoured with a jam tin lid had dissipated, was fine. The second was more careful, and the last was very careful, approaching peak baby’s bumulosity. Here he is doing the usual Tunisian non smile...

![]({{ '/assets/uploads/2019/02/5D5A20EF-40A5-4459-962E-B9F3A9F13ACF.jpeg' | relative_url }})

We chatted away quite a bit about the tourism possibility of the country, but I finally realised, through the mists of malcomprehension, that those who have left school earlyish may have limited, or at least only rudimentary French. Apparently there is a formal study of it as a second language, and the science and maths classes at high school are in French. Maybe he left school early and would have little use for the language in a little subterranean barberium: so his French could be so heavily accented and nonstandard that this half deaf listener could well wallow in a sea of noncommunication. I have come across a few people who were locals, but who only had Arabic. So I felt a bit better about not getting it all.

There is such power to be had in speaking the language of the country, or even the second language. It opens so many points of exit from the touristsphere.

On the last night I went to the usual restaurant, and announced to the chaps that I was leaving. The meal was fine, some fruit and biscuits arrived as well, and when I paid the bill there were lots of handshakes and hugs and kisses goodbye.

Here are the chaps...

![]({{ '/assets/uploads/2019/02/95BE0B21-7ACC-4B52-9727-003C68B30469.jpeg' | relative_url }})

There is such value to be gained from spending time developing relationships. Being a traveller isn’t just about seeing things and ticking the boxes on the bucket list (I am ashamed to have used that term...sorry). I can imagine some criticism from those who see eating at the same place almost every night as indicating a lack of imagination, but it isn’t about the food...having such a warm and wonderful reception doesn’t happen when you only go there once or twice. Even some of the regulars there, with whom I hadn’t interacted apart from a wave, lined up to shake my hand and wish me well. I can’t imagine the same thing happening with the waiters at the Royal. There is a place that I have eaten at in Istanbul maybe twice a year over the last five years...the waiters there remember and greet me with handshakes and good wishes too. For me, that is a fine reason for travelling.

Well, it’s colder than a well-digger’s bum here in instanbul. On with the coat and scarf!

Have a good week,

Tex back in Turkey.
