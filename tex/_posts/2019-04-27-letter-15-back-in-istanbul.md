At the end of the last letter I was still in Sevilla; the threatened rain did eventuate in some massive downpours and street coating hail, so the processions were definitely off. The weather did brighten up though, as I sped through the countryside on the high speed train (it didn’t feel like it was going very fast, but we got to Madrid in a couple of hours, so it must have been).

It was like coming home to get to back to the Airbnb and to be greeted with a hug and a kiss from the owner, and then to go back to the bar over the road, the place where I had spent time waiting to get into the Airbnb 5 weeks before. The young barman with whom I had been chatting then, who had shown me the tricks with the smoke and who had taken me to the Airbnb and found the key and let me in, greeted me as soon as I came in the door with open arms this time and ‘Eh senor Terry! Que tal!’.

There were some little Easter Sunday processions that happened in Madrid, but they were very local affairs compared with the extravaganzas in Sevilla. In the one around the corner from the Airbnb there was just one float, of a long haired Jesus, a biggish band but no penitents...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/04/9368E143-567F-4FDA-9D71-E5A817B473E4.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Then on Monday I flew back to Istanbul.

The new, biggest-in-the-known-universe, airport has opened in Istanbul, and that’s where we landed. To say it is big, and bear in mind that only one third of it has been built, is an understatement. From the time of landing to actually arriving at the terminal took over 20 minutes of taxiing, and the walking to get to the passport control required a packed lunch. The chaos outside, where the lines of taxis and cars and buses to pick up the weary travellers jostled for room, was much the same as the old one. One other problem is that the airport is 55 km from the city, so you need to have packed the second lunch to get there.

Since then I have been doing the rounds of spending time with the good friends whom I will not see for another year or more.

The first was to see Saleh, the young Syrian refugee guy who is now selling towels in a shop in a tourist street. I met him a couple of years ago selling spices in a different shop in the same street. He and his mum and dad came here 4 years ago, and have been accepted by the USA as refugees, and they are waiting, and waiting, for the thing to happen. His dad was there too, so we spent and hour talking. He was an English teacher in Aleppo, and his mum taught Maths. Here we are...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/04/1040FD04-76D5-4954-AE4B-39DB961CFEFF.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The dad, Jelal, told me his story...when he was younger he applied for refugee status in Belgium because he hated the dictatorship of Assad, the father of the present president. He was granted asylum (this was over 30 years ago) and all was well. But then his parents begged him to come back, so he did, and at the airport he was arrested as a spy and a traitor and flung into prison for the next 8 years, where he was tortured and bashed repeatedly. He showed me some scars, and told me of the schizophrenia he now suffers from, with only the medication saving him from becoming different personalities (Hitler, Jesus, Mahommed). After his release he married and had the one son, Saleh, 22 years ago.

Aleppo has been bombed into oblivion, everything they had there has been destroyed, and so they left. His face was very serious as he related all this, but now and then he would break into an enormous smile.

Then later in the afternoon I headed to the Asian side of the city to spend some time with Sertaç, the Turkish online tutor that I had several years ago and with whom I have become the best of friends. He recently married his Russian girlfriend, and they are both the best of people. Here we are having breakfast...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/04/BA622DCF-7C1E-4786-9C99-050E9C437CDB.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Then on Wednesday I got the bus to visit another longtime friend (over 13 years we calculated) up on the Black Sea. He is the guy who lost all his family in the earthquake of 1999 and who was pulled from the rubble after 36 hours, who became a humanities teacher, working in a Fetullah Gulen school, who ended up in prison as a ‘terrorist’ because of that, after the coup attempt from 3 years ago, and who now is married, has two little daughters and works in his fatherinlaw’s menswear shop. He is such a good man.

The bus trip was supposed to take 3 hours (according to the timetable) so I had expected to arrive at 7.45pm, just as the shop was about to close and all would be well. Well...3hours turned into 4+and it transpired that my friend was to play a soccer match at the stadium (a once a week indoor soccer match that happens every week) at 9pm. Bloody hell!

So text messages were frantically relayed, screen shots of the progress on Google Maps, then the instruction to get the bus to stop at a big hotel at the edge of town, rather than going out to the bus station which is miles away. So I asked the conductor if it was possible, in my best Turkish, dreading that he would ask me to clarify, but he just nodded. As we approached the hotel a pair of women in the opposite seats saw that I was getting a bit toey, so one yelled out at the driver ‘Anadolu Otelde inecek var. Yabancı’ which means ‘someone wants to get off at the Anadolu hotel. A foreigner’. The last word opens all sorts of doors. The conductor shepherded me off the bus, pointed out where the hotel was, shook my hand, wished me good fortune and a long life (I assume...the Turkish was very fast) and there was Kadir. Five minutes later he was in the soccer gear and the match was on. Whew! There he is in the blue er shorts. I’m was the only spectator, but armed with a can of beer and a packet of peanuts I was fine.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/04/60F0CEC6-C7FF-49FB-9370-70058D96EA08.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The teams of 7 play every week, and the matches go for an hour, non stop. They don’t have a referee...if you do a foul you own up and that’s that!

After 10pm we got back to his place, his wife and kids were at a friend’s place, and we tucked into grilled fish and salad...

Opposite the shop is a metal work place...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/04/73025810-BF55-4B41-B883-11EA18AB9265.jpeg' | relative_url }})

I had a chat with the bloke, not shown, and couldn’t help noticing that he was lame. I remembered that the Greek god of the forge and blacksmiths, Hephaestus (corresponds with the Roman god Vulcan, hence volcanoes) had a bung leg. Apparently, in the Bronze Age metal workers often added arsenic to copper to make a form of bronze, and this led (I almost wrote lead) to poisoning, resulting in lameness. As we as humans tend to create gods in our own image (a bearded king on the throne, a guy with blue skin who plays the flute, a voluptuous chick with gauze floating around) the god of blacksmiths was lame, or, contrariwise, the lame god was assigned to blacksmiths. So I was burning to know if that was the source of this man’s problem (yes I know we are not in the Bronze Age, but...) so I asked Kadir, and the cause was a car accident. Tragic either way.

A bit of a linguistic aside here. I had always thought that the word ‘chick’ to describe a woman was short for ‘chicken’ and so I didn’t use it all that willingly. But since I have been studying Spanish, I find that the word come from ‘chica’, a totally respectable word for woman. A bloke, in the same linguistic furrow, is a ‘Chico’. So Chico Marx is one thing, and a Chiko Roll is something else.

On Friday night I went for dinner and a drink and chat with another friend from 13 years ago. We met on the ferry from Turkey to Italy, to where I was returning after three months in Turkey and to where he was going for a year’s study in an Italian university. We have kept in touch since then and always have a couple of meals together when I’m here. He is a robotics and IT engineer. We had a great time solving the world’s problems...
