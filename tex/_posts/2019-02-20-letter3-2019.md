A week in Tunis, and I am getting used to the way things are done here. There is of course the common humanity deal of returning smiles, following the rituals of greeting and leaving, being considerate, and in the Medina (the bazaar area) using every trick to separate you from your money. But there are other things to get used to...but more on that later.

I am living next to the main drag, a very broad street with a big median plantation...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/02/4FCB9F1D-B31C-4DBB-BBA2-C50F6D7D8E5D.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The weather is excellent; cool nights, warmish days, clear skies and niceish air. I suspect that the air is filled with dust though, as the cars are almost all dusty, and my nose runs a lot. I think my feet smell too, but I am on the other side of the world, so I should expect some inversion. Still we are on the edge of the Sahara, and water is scarce.

This main thoroughfare ends in a square, from which radiate the alleyways of the souk, the covered bazaar.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2019/02/9D798890-3F56-48B4-97CF-1BECEBB488BE.jpeg' | relative_url }})

It goes forever, narrow cobbled alleyways, with shops on either side, many selling the same thing. The traders I spoke to tell me that after the massacre on the beach a couple of years ago, the tourist trade dried up, and I do notice that there aren’t tour groups of Chinese anywhere. Nor are there many European faces to be seen.

The French influence is very obvious. Along parts of the main drag (named after  Habib Bourguiba, the first president and creator of modern Tunisia) there are sidewalk cafes, just like in Paris, breakfast at these places is a croissant, orange juice and a coffee, signs are in Arabic and French, almost everyone I have spoken to does it in French...and the feel of the place is quite cosmopolitan. The frogs did own the place from 1881 until 1956. In 2011 there was a revolution which was pretty nasty, and the corrupt government overthrown. There are police on the street now, fag in one hand, machine gun in the other, but everything seems to be stable.

I had breakfast at one place, and was asked if I wanted the coffee ‘espresse’ or ‘direct’. Well, I knew the first, but the second looked something new, so I opted for that. Hmm, it seems that café direct is the local appellation for a café latte. It was fine.

There is quite a bit of Christian architecture on the main drag. There is a huge Catholic Church there, built relatively recently (late 19th century) in a gothic revival/Islamic/ Roman/syncretist sort of way. The interior is stunning...

The church is dedicated to St Vincent de Paul, so maybe that him, but my first impression was that it was the big G.

There doesn’t seem to be any religious tension here between Christians, Jews and Muslims, and one bloke in the bazaar proudly told me that there was a Jewish person in the parliament... but no Christians. They are tiny minorities though. I asked him is there were any atheists, but he didn’t seem to get the concept.

I read somewhere that  Habib Bourguiba, the founder, had similar attitudes to the place of religion as that other great nation builder, Ataturk...your religion was your business, and it had no place in the government. So there is no religious requirement for public service for example, except that it is the law that the president has to be a muslim, a bit like the ruler of the UK has to be an Anglican. There is no Sharia law, women have equal rights. However, it is still a blokes world!

There is a huge and very old mosque in the centre of town, but the inner prayer hall is only for muslims. But you can get up to a terrace on top and overlook it. Here I am with the mosque...

Food is very cheap...the breakfast cost around $2, but wine and beer cost a bit more. I have been to the same restaurant, which is next door to where I’m living, several times. It’s a fish place, and the food is excellent. The waiters greet, shake hands and smile and remember what I’ve eaten before, so it feels comfortable.

The whole thing, with a half bottle of wine cost around $14, and the wine was half of that.

The only disadvantage with eating in restaurants here is that there is no antismoking legislation, so everywhere smells like a 1980s staffroom...and most of the blokes do it.

Another example of the cheapness is the excellent shave I had in a dingy, subterranean barbershop...it cost a bit over a dollar!

The traffic is fairly constant, but not very fast. There aren’t lane markings much either, so it is a bit like dodgem cars. Pedestrian lights either don’t work, or are ignored, and you get across by walking boldly out when there is a soupçon of a gap, and the drivers are trusted to do the avoiding. I tend to scuttle in the wake of someone bolder than I am, trusting in local guts rather than my own.

The money system takes a bit of getting used to.

The dinar, which is worth about 50c, is subdivided into 1000 parts. So the half dinar coin is 500 milim. Thus prices are written as 24.500. And that gets read out as vingt quatre, cinq cents= twenty four, five hundred. It requires a bit of adjustment. The numbers are really hard to find on the coins too, which confuses this little traveller. I suspect that the 1000 subdivision came about because, upon gaining independence from France in 1956 the currency became the dinar, defined as being 1000 French francs. Times have changed, but the currency lingers on.

My French is holding up very well. There have been all sorts of discussions with bazaar guys and others, and I manage quite well. I do get asked if I’m French or Belgian or Canadian, I suppose because the idea of a French-speaking Australian is too far outside the box.

Enjoy life...that’s what it’s for

Terry
