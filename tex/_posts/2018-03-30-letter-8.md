hi,

In the last few days of the stay in Izmir I did the rounds of the favourite haunts to say goodbye to the waiters and other chaps.

In the Kardelen restaurant, where I’ve had lunch a couple of times a week, they suggested I should come back next year, and said all the right things...I assume anyway. Much of it was as impenetrable as it always has been, but they seemed to agree with my suggestion that I could be back next year. Inşallah=I hope so.

Here is the pizza maker, the Sylvester Stallone look alike, who talks to me, but does it in such a way that I have no idea what he is saying. I suspect he is making fun of me and my incomprehension, because the others laugh a lot, but I’m not one to be paranoid about such things, so I laugh along as though I know what is happening. The other guy is the owner/head bod, whose vocal delivery would leave a machine gun for dead, but has a highly infectious enthusiasm in everything he does...

*(photo missing: 90990DDE-E4E6-4A9D-BCF6-6BC724F3DF5B-1024x680.jpeg)*

I had had the soup and salad, then the photos and farewell speeches, and I swanned out of the place. It was only when I was 20 m down the road that I realised I hadn’t paid, so I wheeled round and started to stride back. The waiter guy, (I did ask their names earlier on, but they have gone the way of so much else) was about to get on his motor scooter to deliver an order, but when I blurted out the hastily prepared apology  he laughed and told me it was on the house. I suppose when you deal with the owner that thing is possible.

At the fish place I go to  the same farewell-come-back-soon speeches happened, but I didn’t get a photo, and I did remember to pay.

They have a nice name to describe the cook, or the head whatever, and that is ‘usta’. So you say ‘çok teşekkür ederim usta, eline sağlık...thanks boss, health to your hands’. Hmm, it does sound like something a health inspector might say, but it it is what you say instead of ‘garblaments to the chef’.

There is always someone here who will help out when you get into a muddle, or so it seems. I was in the queue at a cash register in a shirt shop and got talking to a Turk, whose English was excellent. He was in front of me in the queue, and after he left I was next. The woman took the shirt then asked me something....was it ‘do you have a loyalty card?’ ‘do you want this gift wrapped?’ ‘are you paying cash?’...I will never know, but as I impersonated a stunned mullet, the guy behind me said ‘yabancı’ (=foreigner), the woman smiled and nodded knowingly and everything was solved. I thanked him in Turkish, exchanged a few pleasantries, in Turkish, and was on my way. Maybe a yabancı, but able to exchange pleasantries like a native!

Another place I ate at several times is called Kırk Yıl=  40 years. What the significance is I don’t know, but it is run by three women who are massively charming and welcoming. My first foray included...’what is there without meat?’....and they remembered thereafter, and when I went there for breakfast the main woman asked if an egg would be ok, presumably referring to my dietary peccadillos. Today I had lunch there, sitting on the terrace with the glorious sun doing its vitamin d trick through my shirt, and eating this...

The tomato the tomato and green chilli thing is pretty self explanatory, the white stuff is yogurt, opposite is some eggplant, smokey and delicious, and the two rissole-like jobs are zucchini fritters, flavoured with dill, a herb that is ubiquitous in Turkish cooking.

There is a ferry which take one from where I am in Bostanlı to Konak on the other side of the bay. It takes about 15 mins to do the trip, and costs less than a dollar. The view of the hills on the way is great, and ever changing. This is what it looked like at 5pm on Thursday afternoon...

*(photo missing: 5A76357B-A0FC-47D9-9B9E-ACBD5D068DF3-1024x768.jpeg)*

Near the ferry terminal at Konak, where that clock tower, which’s photo appeared a few letters ago, there are some paved and shaded areas where, among other things, there are buskers. On Thursday one group really stood out.

*(photo missing: 7BAE1027-3909-40ED-91DB-C7AC392DC7F0-1024x768.jpeg)*

The wind instrument being held by the guy in the front is an oboe-like affair with a really piercing tone and a bacchanalian timbre. The pounding cross rhythms of the drum, and the frenzied wailing of the woodwind made for a terrific sound, and when they launched into the Izmir March, a song that celebrates Ataturk, and which is hearable every day here, the crowd, and I, erupted into applause. It’s a pity I didn’t make a recording of it, but believe me, it was high octane music. Here’s a link to the song sung by a more sedate performers on YouTube...

<https://youtu.be/aqFNCl9Wcyc>

The use of the song is very political, with emphasis on Ataturk, contrary to the policy of the government which is undoing many of the reforms to Turkish society that he made a century ago.
