The flight from Jaipur to Delhi was pretty uneventful.

There had been some confusion when, on the night before, an email arrived saying the flight was changed from 7.20 to 8.30 am. Great I thought...but 30 mins later another arrived saying back to 7.20am. Hmm. I hoped that their air navigation had more planning to it! All was well though. We took off as the sun was rising through the fog and filthy air, and touched down 45 mins later, through some more fog and filthy air.

Delhi is laid out very well. There are very wide streets, and the traffic seems so much less chaotic than that which I had experienced further east.

I have used the very extensive metro system a few times. The ticketing is so easy. You buy a smart card at the window, you tap on when you enter, and tap off when you exit. The cost depends on the distance, and has been a few tens of cents for each of my trips. You put more money if you want to at a window, or on a machine. How or why the planners in Melbourne ever came up with the MYKI Card and its smart-arsed complexity is difficult to fathom. The Uber system is great too; some of the cars are a bit battered but who cares, and for a reasonably sized trip it can be $1 to $2.

In the centre of the city, Connaught Place, I succumbed to the repeated blandishments of one of the many shoeshine guys,who quoted me 40 rupees for the two shoes (80c) so away we went...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/02/img_2170.jpg' | relative_url }})

He found that the insoles were pretty worn, so replaced them with new ones. How much, I had inquired just before the said soles were inserted...whatever you like he said...hmm always a danger signal. So when all was finished he said the price...850 rupees! Clearly an ambit claim, as that’s about $17. So I replied that he had quoted 40, and then something for the soles...that made 100 ($2) in my estimation. He remonstrated feebly, so I gathered that he had done well enough.

I had a spectacular meal here on the day I arrived, in a vaguely up-market place. It was the thali, again, but with parathas, which have the texture of a hot croissant, curries, rice, yoghurt, a sweet dish and a fresh lime soda for less than $8.

The airbnb is ok. It’s in a posh suburb called Greater Kailash. The room is big, the bathroom very plush, all is quiet, but I have had very little interaction with the family. It feels strange after the intensity of the previous places I have stayed in, but that’s the way of the world. Judging places based on your expectations rather than what they themselves have to offer is the road to needless disappointment.

Another religious place I went to was the Sikh temple called Gurudwara Bangla Sahib...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/02/54B68678-1400-40F2-8D8A-EA156BD85F36.jpeg' | relative_url }})

It’s a huge marble place with a prayer hall where the gurus’ writings are continually chanted, with drum of infinitely pleasing rhythmic flexibility, and an accordeon-like drone job, and a big square pool (80mx80m I estimated) outside. The place is so clean and peaceful. But no photos allowed inside.

Sikhism started in the 15th century and has as its tenets (according to Wikipedia), ‘faith and meditation on the name of the one creator, divine unity and equality of all humankind, engaging in selfless service, striving for justice for the benefit and prosperity of all and honest conduct and livelihood while living a householder's life.’

‘Sikhism rejects claims that any particular religious tradition has a monopoly on Absolute Truth.’

‘Sikhs believe that upon death one merges back into the universal nature, just as a drop of rain merges back into the ocean. Individuality is lost. Sikhs do not believe in heaven or hell. Heaven can be experienced by being in tune with God while still alive.

Sounds pretty good to me.

Another place I visited was the Lotus Temple of the Bahá’ís.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/02/30A5B4DF-121D-45E7-81CF-6E43ADFF17B0.jpeg' | relative_url }})
![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/02/6004D48D-9157-4A8C-9A7D-CF4DA0686921.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The imagery is of the Lotus which lives under the mud, but then the flower emerges, pure and beautiful. So the human can rise from the sea of prejudice, intolerance and discord and become purified. The Bahá’í religion started in mid 18th century with a prophet who who radically developed a religion whose aim was to ‘inspire individuals and communities as they work to improve their own lives and contribute to the advancement of civilisation’. Their tenets are expressed on a plaque at the entrance...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/02/1A1EA49E-374E-4CD5-A2D6-92E7167F884B.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Inside the temple there are 9 huge arches supporting the soaring pointed dome, and other arches further out which are windowed and look over the manicured gardens. There are no pious objects, just the plain light grey concrete. There is seating for hundreds of people. The place is calm and quiet and just to sit and be still was a rare treat.

They have no clergy; their condemnation of all forms of superstition, and their devotion to science and the search for truth, means that they have none of the miracles and trickery and promises of eternal damnation or heaven that other faiths have in order to gain and keep converts. They aren’t constrained by ancient texts that get understood as historical reality, and consequently they don’t have the supernatural baggage that clouds the fundamental message. For them religion is about the development of the person, not about explaining where the universe came from, or why childbirth is painful, or where rainbows come from.

I had a long talk with one of the volunteers in the information centre. It was most enlightening. The temples here and elsewhere are for all faiths, for everyone in search of meaning.

It too sounded pretty sensible.

I have been rereading George Orwell’s 1984, and the idea of Big Brother always watching and knowing your inner thoughts, and the idea of ‘thoughtcrime’, the mere action of having a disapproved-of thought being a capital offence, reminded me of some other religions as well. I imagine that Sikhism and Bahá’ís teach you how to respond to such thoughts, and suggest to you moral criteria to judge them, not condemn you for being a fallen creature in need of salvation.

After a couple more days in Delhi I’m off to Istanbul...on the 14th Feb (that date will, for me, forever be associated with the change to decimal currency in Australia). It will be a shock to the system: temps here get to early twenties and the sky is cloudless (although not smogless). In Istanbul it struggles to get to 10 degrees, and there is snow and rain. Gasp. After a week or so it’s off to Spain and the mid 20s again.

Heavy thoughts this time, but India does that to you. I’m so glad I came.
