Hello everyone,

I’m in Afyon now, or Afyonkarahisar to give its proper name. Here’s a map to put this part of the trip into perspective...

*(photo missing: 759A4B8F-D1F2-45B6-B17B-AC8B418DE0D5-1024x768.jpeg)*

The Afyon part means opium, because they grow it here, and the Kara= black and hisar=fortress, because they have the remains of a fortress (now no longer black, but well let that slip by) perched high on a  200+m volcanic plug that dominates the town.

*(photo missing: 7599DA8F-FD0B-4C08-AF26-AE1E23A78AAE-e1523825993254-225x300.jpeg)*

But, back to Isparta for a moment...

I had thought that the barber in Antalya worked hard on shaving me...well, Hakan the barber in Isparta, the guy I went to before catching the bus to Afyon, did the virtuoso performance. The soaping up took longer than usual, and he massaged the suds into my jaw and neck as well. The actual shaving was a real Michelangelo affair, then he brushed powder over the shaved areas, to dry them I surmised, then did it all again with an electric razor!

Next followed the singeing of the errant hairs. This often happens, so don’t be alarmed. He has a bit of wire with cotton wool on the end, he dips it into metho, or cologne maybe, and sets it alight, then whacks it against the ears to burn off anything that’s growing there. Straight after each whack he puts out the burning flesh, if any, with his free hand. Hmm. The place did smell like a barbecue afterwards.

Next I was doused in cologne which found out every little scratch, and the neck and head, then shoulders, back and finally arms got a massage. Finally some moisturiser was rubbed into the gleaming and baby’s bummified cheeks. A cup of tea and a bit of a chat later I was on my way. Wow!

The hotels I stayed in in the two places were at the ends of the hospitality spectrum.

In Isparta, I was in a  big joint with 114 rooms, a foyer of unimaginable faux Louis XV kitschness, a chick on the reception who was efficient enough when I checked in, but who never acknowledged my passing the desk thereafter: even my best response-eliciting smile could not distract her from the computer screen or the mobile phone. Compare that with the Afyon place, 40 odd rooms, a faded dowager-like grandeur, but the guy on the desk, about my age, could not do enough to ensure that I felt at home. I asked about getting some washing done, and two hours after I gave him the shirts and pants, they were returned,  washed and ironed, to my room. I asked directions to a fish restaurant I had discovered, and he sent one of his guys to take me into the street and point me in the right direction...and so on it went. A reception like that has such a powerful effect on ones feelings about the city.

So you might have guessed that I like the place a lot.

The town, Afyon, is on a bit of a cross roads, north-south, and east-west, and has been around since the Hittite Empire 6000 years ago. Since then it has been taken over by every one of the civilisations that has risen and fallen in the area. It’s a volcanic area, there are lots of spas and thermal water treatment centres, it produces a major amount of opium, and refines it to morphine and codeine etc in a massive research and factory complex. Its cherries are famous, and the clotted cream, made from opium-fed buffalo milk, is a standout. They produce eggs by the millions.

The major and decisive battle in the War of Independence, between the revolutionary forces, led by Ataturk, and the Greeks happened here. There is a monument in one of the garden areas here. Instead of the usual group statue of soldiers striving onward and upward, glorifying the sacrifice of the soldiers, there is a 7m high bronze nude (Turkey is not noted for nude anything, apart from Greek and Roman museum pieces) standing over the body of another nude...it is a violent and triumphant sculpture, the like of which I have never seen.

On  Tuesday I climbed up the 600 odd steps to get to the castle at the top of the crag. I last did it when I was here 12 or so years ago, when my legs and heart were in top condition after riding a bike for uncounted km in Italy. This time, after 12 years of relative indolence, the ascent was a bit, no, considerably less impressive. But with a few stops I got to the top.

There isn’t anything much left inside the castle walls.

When I got to the top there was a group of 9 youths, at least one in school uniform, who took a liking to me, helped me up the final steep sections, and invited me up for a drink on the very top of the pinnacle where the flag is. So there I sat being questioned about job, children, country, how I liked Afyon, had I been to the hamam, would I like another glass of Pepsi and so on. I got a potted history of the area from the time of the Hittites, Alexander the Great, Selçuks, Cruasders, Ottomans, and finally to Ataturk’s routing of the Greeks in 1922. I was pretty impressed by the knowledge these kids had, and their enthusiasm and hospitality. It may sound as though I understood everything that was going on, but the reality was a bit less comprehensive, but I caught a fair bit.

Why weren’t they at school ? I hear you ask. I did the same. They had decided to take the day off and have a picnic 200m up. They were around 18, 19 yo.

I caught up with them at a tea place at the bottom of the climb, and joined in with them.

*(photo missing: B6161818-F918-4AA0-8411-33DE7E0E2BF0-1024x591.jpeg)*

When all that was finished they each shook my hand, uttered the usual parting rituals and off they went. It was a nice time.

The last time such a thing happened was when I was in Bergama, 12 years ago. The blokes were a bit older, and the drink was some rotgut red wine. They were impersonating newts when they called me over to join in, and were impersonating even better by the time I left. Pepsi or rough red...not a lot between them.

There are lots of mosques here, so the predawn chorus, not of birds but the muezzins calling the faithful to wake up and pray, around 5.30 am has the texture of a huge orchestra tuning up. Not a lot penetrates the double glazing, but during the other four calls during the day it is attention grabbing.
