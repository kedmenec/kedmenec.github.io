This week has been mainly associated with classes at the school, and getting more validation as a human going forward. The weather has been like a Kerang autumn should be, 22 degrees, sunny and still, in other words excellent. The house I’m in continues to suit me and I am enjoying life here greatly.

Because of some staffing problems at the school on Tuesday and Wednesday, I only had a 90 minute lesson, but caught up the missed time on Thursday and Friday. Thus I had three 90 min classes on each of those days. The brain was pretty numb by the end, but real progress is happening I feel. The teacher sometimes brings in crazy things she has seen about Australia in the Murdoch-like newspapers here, and they are the stimulus for talking. Things like; there is a Mt Disappointment, that Melbourne was called for a short time Batmania, (true, according to Wikipedia, and another contender was Barebrass!), that wearing pink shorts on Sundays was forbidden by law...it has been a good education for me too.

I went to the covered bazaar here on Wednesday. It is called Kemeraltı, which means ‘under the belt’, I think as a reference to a Roman aqueduct that used to pass over the area. There were some blokes there that I spent time with last year, sitting in their shops chatting, drinking tea, and I went to see if the chatting could continue.

The first encounter was with the shirt guy. I bought some clothes from him last year, and the year before too. As I walked past the shop he was occupied with a customer, but two steps later I heard him shout ‘ merhaba Avustralyalı’ and there he was all beaming and ready for a chat. He talks at full volume all the time, which is good for this hearing-challenged foreigner, he seemed to be talking a bit slower than the usual machine gun delivery, but that may be a sign that I’m getting better at it, and he had had all his top teeth out. Hmm. Turkish has a lot of words that end in -sh, and for that reason I was pleased I was wearing a jacket. A glass of tea later, and introductions to everyone within shouting distance I was on my way again, validated.

Next I called into a restaurant I’d spent time at, which is in a tree covered square outside a big mosque, the Hisar mosque, on one side of the bazaar. It’s a very cool place, there are lots of big shady trees and in the centre stalls selling plants and seedlings. One of the waiters there, named Murat, has greeted me over the last two years when I’ve gone there, and this time it was no different... the big smile, the handshake and the kiss kiss. Even the boss came out and joined in...he’s a shiny domed type named Kahraman, which means ‘hero’. There was lots of chatting and joking, in Turkish and English, and the soup afterwards was great.

The third guy I wanted to see was Cem the jacket guy, but his shop had turned into a clothing place, and his whereabouts is unknown to me.

On Thursday night I went to a concert in the concert hall which is just around the corner from where I live. There was a soprano, as well as a couple of other ‘guests’ and a smallish orchestra containing clarinet, sax, violin, double bass, trombone, guitar, drums and piano. She sang songs from Broadway shows, and it was excellent. Her English pronunciation was fine, except for ‘th’. Turkish doesn’t have that sound, and they find it very hard to make, as do most other language-speakers. So every ‘the’ was ‘duh’ and every ‘there’ was ‘dere’. I did wonder how she would go with ‘anaesthetist’, but even English speakers make a mess of that one. Hearing songs by Cole Porter, Gershwin, Bernstein et al juxtaposed with some by Andrew Lloyd-Webber, made me realise how melodically and lyrically vapid the latter are.

On Saturday I got the train to Selçuk, a town about 2hrs south, and within spitting distance of the archaeological site of Ephesus. I’d been to Ephesus several times before, so I gave that a miss, but instead wandered around the quiet streets revisiting places I’d been to when I stayed in the town 12 years ago. The tourist trade has dried up, and as one bloke I talked to said ‘you have to use a torch to find any Aussies here now!’ So the carpet and souvenir places are doing it very tough. The huge market however had lots of people...locals buying food and clothes.

I chatted for a half hour or so with one guy, in a carpet and ceramic shop, who showed me some kilims, and explained how he sold only carpets and kilims a few years ago, but recently had to change his business plan and move into jewellery and ceramics, because of the change in tourist numbers. He said that even in summer he sometimes goes for a couple of days at a time without having a customer. He was a charming bloke...middle aged and simpatico, and I really felt sorry for his plight.

There used to be lots of Australian backpackers come through, going on the path from Gallipoli, Ephesus, Kuşadası then south to the fleshpots of Bodrum and the Mediterranean coast, so there are Australian reminders everywhere in Selçuk. I stopped outside the ‘Boomerang Pension and Carpet ‘ place, and chatted for a while with the owner. Last time I was here I did the same thing with this guy’s brother. Back then, we, or more specifically I, struggled along in Turkish until he asked me where I was from. When I told him his face lit up and he shouted ‘F... me!!! I worked for 15 years in a factory in Fawkner’ in an accent so broad and so studded with expletives that I had no doubt that what he said was true! Today’s bloke was his brother Ali, whose language was a good deal less colourful.

A few steps on I revisited the Homeros Pension, the place I stayed at back then, and got invited in by the boss to sit with him and three of his friends for some wine and cheese. The boss spoke good English, but we did it in Turkish some of the time, and he was very careful and slow in the way he talked to me, so I understood most of what he said. That is a bit of a change from the usual scenario where they assume, based on your saying of hi, how are you, that you can actually speak the lingo, so go at it hammer and tongs.

The museum in the town is a pretty nice place with many of the exhibits coming from the nearby site of the Temple of Artemis, which was one of the Wonders of the Ancient World, but which has disappeared since. Paul got himself into trouble in Ephesus 2000 odd years ago, peddling an alternative vision of reality to the worship of Artemis, and ended up in gaol for creating a disturbance. There are two famous statues of the goddess in the museum, she being one manifestation of the Earth Mother, Fertility, and so on, Here she is, with what appears to be multiple busty substances, quite suitable for her area of influence. But they are thought to be bull’s dangly bits, or some type of gourd, both having connections with fecundity.

It was a great day in the town, and I felt very validated.

That’s it for now. Into the second week of studies, and I am game to ask the shop keeper to slow down, so I am doing a lot less nodding. There is a question particle, a little syllable that tells you that the sentence was a question (sorry about the mansplaining) which most often comes at the end of a sentence, and can sound like mi, or misin, or other variants. I live in fear of hearing that among the hail of machine gun fire, because a response is called for, and the nodding just won’t cut the mustard. I am less afraid now!

All the best to youse,  
Terry.

In Turkish the c is pronounced as je, so Terence comes out as terenje, which sounds a lot like tencere, which means a saucepan. Good for a laugh sometimes.
