hi again,

Just one week left of the course.

My teacher was sick on Tuesday (20th), so Wednesday and  Thursday’s classes ended up being 4 and a half hours long, the latter parts of which were underpinned by rusty grinding of the brain cells, and a fervent longing for the clock to strike 3. I can now understand the glazed looked that kids had after a double period of maths on Friday afternoon, not really giving a rat’s about the square on the hypotenuse, or anywhere else for that matter.

I haven’t said much about the other guy who lives in the house with Maral and Timur...and me. He’s a 40 something yo electrical engineer who is in the airforce and does maintenance on jets and helicopters at the airport here. His name is Murat, and is a splendid fellow. He’s divorced, and it suits him to share the house here for a number of reasons. He has organised stuff for us to do several times and I have enjoyed his company greatly.

Last week he organised a trip to the theatre, but I misunderstood the day, so he went alone, but on Wednesday this week, after many reminders, we met up to see another play in a splendid small theatre in Konak, on the other side of the bay. The play examined the question of whose decision it is to stop treatment in a hospital...the doctors’ or the patient’s.  The patient, a young sculptor gets paralysed from the neck down in a car accident. He doesn’t want to live, there is no improvement possible, he wants to go home and die. The doctor wants to keep him alive in hospital. There are social workers, other doctors, nurses and lawyers all involved, and in a trial at the end of the show we are all invited to vote, yes or no. This decision was incorporated into the last minutes.

The play is by Brian Clark, a British guy, and was put on tv a while back, and called ‘Whose decision is it anyway?’. Here is was translated into Turkish.

I understood very little of the dialogue, but could give a fair description of the story...interesting eh.

Murat’s English is rather rusty, and my Turkish is not really up to discussing the nature of existential crises, but it was a good night.

On the Monday night we went to a concert in the nearby hall where the Karşıyaka Chamber Orchestra and some soloists did some things by Spohr (not interwoven with Bach and Beethoven!), and by a couple of contemporary Turkish composers, both of whom were in the audience and took bows. The music was great.

I’ve been going to the same barber as last year, and the year before (those of you who are thinking that I am in a rut, and pleased to be, are thinking the same way as I am). This time we are talking lots more, mostly because I can, and he seems to be talking slower now. But I still have difficulty in understanding him, partly due to his rapid and indistinct delivery, and even when I ask for it slower it is still as indistinct. Ah well!

At one time I thought he had asked me about my job (işin) so I launched into a potted history of my past, but toward the end of it, when he had a slightly glazed look, I reran the question in my head (I have become very adept at putting the mouth into automatic pilot while using the brain to do planning, revising etc. it makes no difference to the quality of the vocals, they are still at the far end of incomprehensibility). Whoops...the word was ‘işim’. Not a big difference in sound, but meaning ‘my job’...he was asking about being a barber in Australia. Gasp.

His name is Ahmet, and here’s a pic from 2 years ago. Nothing much has changed. I tend not to chat much when he’s doing the shaving, especially when he’s getting into the nooks and crannies of the dewlap...there’s no knowing what damage could result from the interaction of a sharp cutthroat razor and a rapidly bobbing Adam’s apple!

On Saturday Murat, decided that I needed a trip to the other side of the bay, so we got into his small Renault and headed off through the traffic. It had vague echoes of formula 1 racing, the onboard status check screen suggested that the rear brake lights were nonfunctional and I guess the indicators were the same, as he didn’t seem to be using them. There is a depression in his passenger side floor though...right where the brake pedal would be in a RH drive car. Actually it wasn’t all that scary, but sitting on the RH side with no steering wheel is a bit scary.

We went to the top of the highest (I think) hill behind the city, where the air is clear and cool, everything is green, there is a restaurant, and a spectacular view of the city and bay...

After a coffee that should keep me awake for a week, we rallied down the hill to a fish market specialising area, where a half loaf of bread stuffed with onions, salad and sardines cost around $2. We also drank a deep red drink made from fermented black carrot juice. It’s a bit of an acquired taste, but I managed to have it by halfway through. Next was an archaeological site called Teos. Two thousand years ago this was a religious area filled with temples and that, particularly to Dionysius (who morphed into Bacchus for the Romans). There were lots of fallen columns, foundations, and a couple of vaguely intact amphitheatres...

For adjectives, particularly of colour, Turkish has a way of making them more intense with a prefix. So ‘white’ is ‘beyaz’ but ‘white as snow’ is ‘bembeyaz’, ‘red’ is ‘kırmızı’ but ‘red as red, very red, bloody red’ is ‘kıpkırmızı’ . So the scene there wasn’t just ‘yeşil’, it was ‘yemyeşil’. It’s used for other adjectives too: ‘fresh’ is ‘taze’, but ‘fresh as a daisy, fresh as fresh’ is ‘taptaze’. Theres no strict rule on how to form the prefix, you just have to learn the words. I’ve tried making them up: ‘güzel’ (beautiful) intensifying into ‘güpgüzel’, but the spell checker goes into a frenzy, and the Turks give a pitying smile.
