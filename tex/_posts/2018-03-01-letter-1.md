The trip to the other side of the earth has started again, and in the last week I’ve gone from reasonably hot and dry Victoria to reasonably hot and wet Kuala Lumpur, and finally to unreasonably cold and wet Istanbul.

KL was great again. As will be a recurring motif in these letters, the smile of recognition from the fake watch seller in the street, or the continuation of the the conversation started six months ago with the Bangladeshi masseur, or the warm smile and handshake from most of the waiters in the Indian curry house, is really heartwarming. The reality of being anonymous in a foreign city is all very well, but to go to the same curry house that one ate at several times in previous visits, and have the usual order remembered(one egg roti, one orange juice and a coffee for breakfast for example) and suggested before the greeting ritual had finished, is really..er...validating as a human going forward. (Sorry about that....I’ve been listening to Michaelia Cash too much)

One day I went on a trip to an island called Crab Island (something like Pulau Ketam in the local language). It, the trip, entailed a one hour train trip, then 30 minutes in a small ferry. The island is one of many, not coral sand and waving coconut palms, but mud heaps, scarcely above sea level and held in place by mangroves and other jungular species. But it is the mud flats where the crabs are caught. The land is submerged at high tide, so the houses are all on stilts. The major attraction for the tourist is the plethora of restaurants selling crabs, prawns and other fish, and on the weekends it is all on for young and old. On the Monday when I went the places were pretty much deserted as was the village.

There are no roads, only walkways, some concrete, but the minor paths to the off-road houses wood. There are no motorised vehicles on the island, but there are electric pushbikes and motor scooters.

It is pretty perilous strolling along the walkways, gazing here and there, because there are no railings, and a plunge into the muddy crab infested water could be terminal. But this is exacerbated by the silent and deadly cyclers who move at breakneck speed, and who are undetectable when they approach from behind. When the crowds are there on the weekends it must be something else.

There are little Buddhist shrines everywhere, some big ones too, and the smell of incense is a splendid counterpoint to the birdsong punctuated silence.

Back in KL I had some good conversations with blokes I have known for a few years there. One had recently changed jobs and is now a housekeeper person in a swish hotel. He works a 12 hour day, the usual situation for many people in KL, and gets about $2.50 an hour...better than his last job! Days off happen when it’s not busy, but they aren’t with pay. Hmm. Life is cheaper here though, or did I mean the cost of living is cheaper?

It was the closing stages of the Chinese New Year celebrations here last weekend, and there were lots of entertainments and fireworks. One was a splendid dragon acrobatic thing..the dragon (in reality two people inside, please pardon the mansplaining) leaped, reared ran and jumped, all the while going from red post to red. It was all accompanied by a band of shrieky and percussion instruments. It was pretty amazing. I took a video of it, but could not find a way to embed it into this document.

On Tuesday I flew to Istanbul, 12 hours of relative comfort (relative to what can set the bar rather low), dressed appropriately, coming from a day where, even at 6 am when I got the train to the airport, the temp was around 27 and the humidity nearing saturation. Once arriving in Turkey, having shuffled along in a queue to get to the passport control, the whole process taking 25 mins, I emerged from the building and realised that I was dressed entirely inappropriately for a raining and freezing Istanbul. A quick ferret in the luggage remedied that. Some friends picked me up and that was a relief.

I’ve had one freezing day here, but the reception from people I’d spent time with a year ago has been very warm. I had breakfast at the cafe I went to lots last time, and it was as though I had never left.

Here’s the breakfast...the pomegranate juice and the menemen , an egg and tomato thing, perilously similar to the ‘mock salmon’ of ones childhood, but with extra garlic and capsicum. It is delicious.

I had a good chat with a couple of blokes in a Turkish delight/tea/assorted traditional aphrodisiac shop. I spent quite a bit of time with them last year, practising the language and just passing the time, and the welcome back was warm. It’s quite convincing when they have remembered lots of details of the last trip, and very validating going forward. And then there were several glasses of tea.

In the Grand Bazaar I did some strolling and caught up with some old friends and chatted with some new people, and drank more tea.

The final interaction of the day was with a barber I have been going to each year when I’ve been here. After having been validated once again, I had the 30 minute treatment of rejuvenation. I suspect the guy may have OCD as the implements of destruction were laid out with military precision, and the attention to detail in the shaving and particularly the hair cutting, was intense. I did feel a congruence with the idea that is floated sometimes about Michelangelo freeing the sculpture hidden within the block of marble...in my case, as the grey and whiteness was clippered off, I felt like the inner Turk was being revealed. He offered me more tea, but my bladder was severely distended already, so I declined.

Tonight I’m having the traditional rakı balık meal with a longtime friend named Buğrahan. The balık means fish, and it will be a whole job, barbecued and delicious, the rakı is the aniseed flavoured liquor, like Ouzo or Pernod, which you drink with water and which is the best social lubricant I know. I expect that we’ll have mezes as well...little plates of dips and things. If I don’t feel too embarrassed at being a food pornographer I’ll put some photos in.

All the best to you all, T .
