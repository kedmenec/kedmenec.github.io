Selam arkadaşlarım,

That means ‘howdy my friends’

I’m in Bursa now, on the west coast, after a pleasant 4 and a half hour bus ride from Afyon. I was a bit sad to leave Afyon, even though I had walked every street, visited every (almost) mosque, climbed every mountain, but I felt very comfortable in the place. The blokes at the hotel looked after me very well...I suspect that they don’t get all that many entertaining foreigners who make an effort with the language and who do greetings and farewells à la turca.

In Afyon I went to the same fish place (one of several in this apparently carnivorous town) each night, and one night I had the anchovies....called hamsi here.

*(photo missing: 153C1F4A-FB79-46FA-8A65-F947F571D68C-768x1024.jpeg)*

The little fish are gutted and decapitated, artfully arranged then quickly fried. They taste mostly of fishy crispness, and are delicious. Well, I surmised that they had been gutted: it would be a pretty labour intensive job. The bottle of red drink on the right is şalgam, the fermented black (!) carrot juice, that is a bit of an acquired taste, but one which I have embraced. I couldn’t find one eatery here that sold booze, so in the absence of any of that, şalgam is the answer. Water is rather insipid with fish, and I had my quota of soft drink for the decade when I was on top of the hill with the young chaps.

I found some interesting signs to do with food ...

In the dining room was this beauty...

*(photo missing: 83233029-93EE-4521-867B-23CA16EF54E5-1024x768.jpeg)*

It says ‘fishing is strictly forbidden’. That translation sounds a bit authoritarian, but I haven’t asked anyone what the flavour of it really is. It’s not enough just to know what the words mean, it’s the tone that is important.

Another sign in the gardens below the violent statue reads...

*(photo missing: AD859FAF-D14E-4F5E-BFE5-0D8129D09EDD-893x1024.jpeg)*

It says ‘the eating of all sorts of seeds with shells, and the throwing of cigarettes butts is forbidden’.

The fag ends part makes sense, especially since just about everyone, well blokes anyway, seems to smoke. I asked the young blokes up the hill if they smoked, and it seemed that most did! The one guy who was doing most of the talking however said that he didn’t, but justified his masculinity by saying that he drank alcohol! I tried to get that into some sort of context, but either I wasn’t making sense, or I didn’t understand the answer.

But what of the seeds?

It seems that the snack of choice to eat while sitting in a park, at the seaside, or on top of a hill is the sunflower seed, still in its husk. They, the locals, are amazingly efficient at getting the seed out using the teeth, and then dropping the rest to the ground. Along the waterfront in Izmir there were small hillocks of the husks, and when I was with the guys up the crag, the sunflowers seeds, rather than chips or cheezels, were the snack du jour. I haven’t mastered the art of eating them without getting a mouth full of husk shrapnel, ...small children can do it effortlessly, so I  maybe should have started practising half a century ago! It’s much like speaking the language. Sigh.

So that’s the problem for the Afyonkarahisar Belediyesi (the Afyon municipality), cigarette butts and sunflower husks.

Here’s a lunch that I had in Afyon.

*(photo missing: 7266594F-DD65-4B89-ADD8-51B233BFF7DA-1024x768.jpeg)*

It is eggplant (patlıcan here) stewed in olive oil, with a green chilli, a tomato and a chip or two. The top left hand corner shows some pickled, somewhat hotter, chillies, and the drink on the right is ayran, a yoghurty drink, the perfect antidote to the chillies.

In towns the size of Afyon, and others, the public transport is mostly by dolmuş. These are minibuses that go along a route specified by cards hanging in the front window. There are designated dolmuş stops, but you can hail one anywhere (I think) and it will stop. There is a fixed fare...in Afyon it was about 80 cents...and when you get on, or later, you pay the driver. He seems generally to be on his mobile (exaggeration alert there) and he steers with his knees as he gets the change. The word dolmüş means ‘stuffed’ which I think refers to an earlier practice of the minibus not starting until it was full of passengers (meaning that there is no timetable) but it could be extended to the potential for continued existence. Traffic is generally quite chaotic, but pretty slow. I get the impression that if you have told the driver where you want to go to he will stop there and tell you. It’s a nice system, better than big and less frequent buses.

The trip in the intercity bus was painless, through broad plains edged by biggish hills, into whose cleavages occasionally nestled small villages of tiled roofed houses, a mosque with a silver dome and an energetic minaret. Google maps, which puts your location onto the map (sorry about the mansplaining again) stopped me asking ‘hey Dad, are we there yet?’ which was a relief for us all. It also really helped when I was on the town bus going from the right-out-of-town bus station into the centre. The problem with buses is knowing when the stop is coming up, or even which stop you want, and the google maps solves all of that. The beauty of trams and trains is that you know what is coming next.

In the evening I felt the need of a shave, so I went to the barber next to the hotel. I was here last year at the end of my little trip around, but stayed in a swankier place just up the lane. And I went to the same barber then too. The men inside greeted me, shook hands asked me how I was...I had the shave, then a proffered  glass of tea...then the barber said...you were here last year, you were staying at the Bursa Palace Hotel. True? All of it was! So we chatted for a while, then I headed off to a cafe up the lane, a place I had eaten at twice a day during my last visit, and had got to know the personnel well.

When I strolled in the cook guy gave a big grin, greeted and welcomed me, then the waiter fronted up, smiling hugely, did the handshake and the kiss kiss, remembered my vegetarianism, and soon I was into it. It is pretty nice to be remembered like this.

The bloke on reception in the hotel here is good value, and seemed relieved when I uttered some Turkish, thus saving him the agony of speaking English. Little did he realise that he had just transferred the agony to me, but he assumed that because I spoke in grammatically correct sentences that I could understand them as well. I did get him to slow down, and the comprehension index rose a little, but still I had to guess from the context and half of the words what was going on. Lots of smiles helps.

That’s it for now.

kendine iyi bak...and look after yourself too
