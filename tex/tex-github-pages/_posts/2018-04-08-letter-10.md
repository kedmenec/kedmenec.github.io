Hi everyone,

The morning that I left Antalya the weather was quite warm, and while the air was cool in the shaded streets, out in the open it wasn’t. The temp was probably around 22. When it is twice that it must be a powerful incentive to hide in the hotel with the aircon  on.

I found a barber in the commercial area, and had the face scraped back to the baby’s bumness that lurks beneath. It’s interesting to compare shaving techniques among barbers. Some do gentle, light strokes, others move swiftly and more intensely. They all have to gently stretch the dewlap to the side so as to get the whiskers that somehow think they are safe clustered on the Adam’s Apple.  But their attention to the definition of the beard and mo has consequences.

In order to get the line straight they need to get down to the individual pixel and this has had the result that the beard and mo have been reducing in size over my time here. So the beard is reduced to a straggle of hairs, and the mo ...

If I was a young black-haired Turk, such an operation would leave me looking very sharp indeed, but as I’m not it all looks pretty untidy. The mo, if black, would have singled me out as a used-car salesman or a not-to-be-tangled-with shiv-toting spiv, but as it is, it looks like I’ve had a glass of milk and haven’t wiped my mouth.

The Antalya barber did interpret my comments on the diminishingness of the mo as an instruction to prune less closely, so now it looks even more milky.

The bus ride north to Isparta was through rocky mountains with views of snow covered ones in the distance.

I’ve tried to find out where the name comes from. An article in Wikipedia says that the Greek spelling has it as Sparta (perhaps they are unwilling to concede that they were booted out of the country a century ago, much like some historically constipated Christians refer to Istanbul as Constantinople, refusing to acknowledge that Constantinople fell to Mehmet the Conqueror in 1453) without the I at the front. So why is it there?

Here’s my analysis...

In original Turkish there are no words that begin with two consonants, and they have difficulty with the pronunciation of imported words that do. Sometimes they just put a vowel between the consonants when they say the word...so ‘plan’ becomes ‘pilan’ and ‘spor’ which is the turkification of the French pronunciation of ‘sport’ gets out as ‘sipor’. I even saw, last year a big banner outside a gym with the word ‘Plates’ on it, which I later realised was ‘Pilates’. They write the word as Pilates, but the sign writer must have reverse pronunciated it to Plates.

The other technique is to put an i in front, in the written word,  so ‘station’ becomes ‘istasyon’ and....you guessed it ... ‘Sparta’ becomes ‘Isparta’. In English we do a similar thing I suspect...special and especial.

Huge language reforms happened here in the 1920s, when the script was changed from Arabic to Latin, and lots of Arabic and Persian words were removed. New words had to be imported to fill the gaps and for new ideas, and they generally came from French, but spelt phonetically. So we have garaj, masaj, deterjan, demokrasi, otel, bulvar...and maybe from English, a jumper is a süveter.

There are some nice things words for new things that are built from Turkish though. A ‘computer’ is a ‘bilgisayar’ where the ‘bilgi’ means ‘information’ and the ‘sayar’ to do with counting. And a ball point pen is a ‘tükenmez kalem’ which translates as an ‘inexhaustible pencil’, which is true for a while anyway.

I felt the change in culture rather keenly when I arrived. This place is not tourist oriented at all, so the sycophantic fawning that I have grown to love in tourist places is sadly lacking. There are no sticky salesmen touting for business on the street for example. But I have managed to warm up the reception at two restaurants.

This place is meat oriented...it seems that those places not selling rose products are kebab joints. But I did find a fish place not far from the hotel.

The first visit was just-another-customerly sort of reception...civil, efficient, but on the second there were smiles and handshakes and suggestions from the menu (not that I understood them in detail). Even the enscarfed woman on the till, who was efficiently  polite last time, was positively beaming the second. The third and last time I went there I had a fishkebab (my word, not theirs) which was lumps of swordfish threaded on skewers and grilled. It was delicious. And the reception by the waiters was very warm as well. It really is the way to go in a strange town...three visits to a restaurant and you’re a valued customer.

A similar story unfolded at a lunch place, but with the added spice that, as I was finishing the lentil soup and salad, I realised I had left the wallet in the hotel. I rapidly rehearsed the explanation, and delivered it as I handed over the 3 lira I had in my pocket, pitifully short of the 5 lira bill. (That’s about $2). So I returned to the hotel, got the wallet, found the cafe again (no mean feat) and paid up. The second visit began with handshakes all around, beaming smiles and a ‘where are you from’ from the waiter. Quite a good technique for recognition perhaps.

They have a swish new ethnographic, kilim and carpet museum here. I followed Mr Googlemap’s instructions on how to reach it, walked for 35 minutes out of town to a ruined industrial site where there wasn’t a museum, then back into town. At the city museum, closed for the next two years for renovations, a guy showed me on a map where the new museum was, so on Sunday I found it, and visited.

At the top is an observation room with good views of the city. There was a bit of morning smog about, and the black spots are the dirt on the window.

*(photo missing: 3AD2F013-7EFC-4848-A8D1-9F73B71E7D85-1024x768.jpeg)*

The area is highly prone to earthquakes, so little remains of any ‘old city’. There are very old churches, (the bishop of here has his name on the list of attendees at the first Conference at Nicea to the northwest, in the 4th century, when the emperor Constantine tried to force some agreed position on the religion in order to sort out the warring sects whose mutually exclusive views on the divinity or otherwise of Jesus was a) causing considerable societal unrest, and b) was in no fit state to become the unifying religion of the empire that he wished for. He managed to get them to write the Nicean Creed, but it didn’t stop the fighting. Eventually one view of the world prevailed and the leaders of the now orthodox belief system set about destroying any opposition, pagan or heretical).

There are also some old mosques, but almost everything is newish, the street plan is rectangular and the feel of the joint, to this traveller at least, is a bit like the main drag of Swan Hill but with five story buildings, more kebab shops and the only Anglo face to be seen is when I look in a shop window.

Tomorrow, Monday 9th, I travel north to Afyon. The name is Turkish for Opium, a major crop here. It was the site of the last major battle when Ataturk beat the opportunistic Greeks who were intent on colonising the country after the defeat of WW1.
