Selamlar,
There is something rather interesting about Bursa compared with, say, Afyonkarahisar. Both places have more mosques (cami in Turkish, pronounced jami, not related to what you wear in bed) per square imam than many other places, so you would expect the 5.30am call to prayer to be a riotously polyphonic affair, and in Afyon it is. In Bursa though only one muezzin does the calling, that’s all there is, all the others in the CBD are silent.
The single call, amazing in its virtuosity and phrase length, and played through relatively nondistorting speakers, comes from the Ulu Cami (=the grand mosque) which is about 100 m away. This place is very grand, built at the end of the 1300s and is surrounded by gardens and the big bazaar.

When the guided tours come in, the light level seems to drop...I suspect it is because all the cameras are sucking up the luminosity. The day I was there was a Friday, and the anniversary of Mohamed’s ascension, and as I passed the mosque during the final call to prayer at around 9 pm, the crowds were huge.
In the middle of the place is a fountain, where the men ritually wash their heads and feet. It is a bit unusual to have it inside, and the sound of the water is is a nice counterpoint to the clicking of the shutters.

*(photo missing: 67C146BF-17B8-433E-9842-2E5ACDB483EB-768x1024.jpeg)*

The sign says...

*(photo missing: 112C19AD-02A4-456B-B5A5-E63D702EC7C9-768x1024.jpeg)*

...which translates as  ‘please don’t wear socks’ and underneath ‘women are forbidden from entering the fountain’. Women have a special part of the mosque, so I guess that they have a special ablution facility as well. The washing place is usually outside, and I have never seen women using it there either.
Here is some splendid calligraphy. This mosque has such calligraphs on every available surface.

*(photo missing: BC05729E-3D53-4EC4-AD81-48DD0243A0AF-928x1024.jpeg)*

The words are some verses from the Koran I suspect, but the letters are arranged artistically, so possibly the meaning can’t actually be descried from looking. I love the flexibility of the lines: it really seems like a physical manifestation of the music of the call to prayer.
The niche that shows the direction of Mecca is particularly ornate..

*(photo missing: A6D004A0-CEDD-4563-B720-C2F541F83838-726x1024.jpeg)*

For the weary tourist, a visit to a mosque is a great relief. You have to take your shoes off, the carpet is usually thick and soft and the place is cool and usually quiet. It very relaxing to sit on the floor and let the time pass, while contemplating the infinite.

I have patronised the favourite restaurant here (Hacibey Lokantası) several times, and been welcomed warmly each time. Here are two of the staff and me...

*(photo missing: 5F7D7F59-FD15-44AB-9419-2CEC1C8ECFDD-300x240.jpeg)*

One of the waiters is a tiny man, less than a metre and a half tall I’d reckon. He is a very welcoming and friendly chap, really good at his job, and it is so good to be able to see the waiter eye to eye when you are sitting down, rather than crane the neck backwards and look up their noses.

On Friday night I had dinner with a turk whom I’d met on the Bursa to Istanbul ferry last year. He teaches English to middle school kids and we had vaguely kept in touch on Facebook. It was good evening of sorting out the problems of the world, well, at least the problem of whether to have dessert or not.
On Sunday while I was strolling around one of the hans...what’s a han you ask? The were places where traders, hundreds of years ago, would bring their things for sale, a big courtyard enclosed two story cloister-like galleries. This particular one has been renovated and now has tea cafes in the courtyard as well as some big trees, and shops selling scarves and silk things in the galleries. Bursa was the end of the Silk Road I think I read.
