Varanasi, on the Ganges river, and most holy place for Hindus and reportedly the oldest inhabited city in the world, is quite a change from Kolkata. The streets are still decayed and wildly overpopulated with cars, motorbikes, pushbikes, tuktuks, rickshaws, cows and people. The mangy street dogs are still here and the heaps of unfinished stuff everywhere, but the air is marginally fresher, the temperature a little lower and the pace of life, at least where I am, a little less frantic. And the Ganges itself is a most pleasant place to walk along.

I went for a wildly overpriced boat ride with a young bloke, to look at the ghats (meaning the banks, but the sides are massively built up and the areas paved). There are palaces and temples and hotels and crematoria.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1936.jpg' | relative_url }})

For Hindus, being cremated (either on wood fired pyres or in a industrial crematorium) then have the ashes dispersed in the Mother Ganges, is most important. The woodfired ones go 24/7.

During the winter here there are migrating Siberian Seagulls which nest on the floodplane on the other side of the river. The guys in the boats call them, with the sound a bit like oi,oi,oi, and the birds come in flocks to pick up the specially bought food.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1939-1.jpg' | relative_url }})

The boatman told me a bit about his life, and in an explanation for the bloodstains on his sleeve told me of his anguish at the breakup with his girlfriend a couple of years ago. Here he is, one hand on the tiller and the other on the throttle of the motor.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1937-2.jpg' | relative_url }})

He said that when he gets depressed, (because of the separation) he drinks, then cuts his arms. On the inside of his forearms were scars and still-oozing slashes. One he showed me needed 8 stitches! I did make the suggestion that maybe it was time to move on he replied that there was no moving on for him, only despair.

I did think that, faced with such obsession, the girl did the smart thing, but I kept that to myself. At one stage he had to disentangle something from around the propellor of the boat, putting his arms in the less than salubrious water. Gasp I thought...where is the dettol?

The swastika is a major good symbol for Hindus, and it is part of the decoration of many buildings and temples.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1962.jpg' | relative_url }})

It is unfortunate that the nazis coopted the sign so that it is a symbol of evil for us in the west at least. The symbol on the left is that of the universal sound ‘om’ which features in much Hindu chanting (see below).

Nearby my airbnb is this...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1933.jpg' | relative_url }})

I did feel like setting a bomb off underneath it, but that’s as far as it went.

On Thursday morning, very early, I went on a walking tour with a splendid guide called Kuldeep. We went to a major ritual that happens every morning at 6 ish, where the sunrise and the river are saluted and welcomed by 7 monks performing rituals with fire and other symbolic things to the sound of cymbals and bells and chanting (lots of oms there). It was most atmospheric. Unfortunately the smog was so thick that we didn’t get to see the sun until later in the day. After that ceremony there is a half hour concert of classical Indian music, then a group yoga free for all.

I did have a short video of some of the ceremony, but I couldn’t get it to load.

The tour also includes a chai crawl, having the sweet spicy delicious milky stuff from several stalls on the way. One such stall is a major place where opinion makers come in the morning to discuss the day’s events, and drink chai. The owners of these places, and other little shops selling stuff, sit cross legged like this guy...all day! Kuldeep is the guy on the right.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1967-1.jpg' | relative_url }})

Next he took us to a gymnasium where blokes go to wrestle, build up stamina etc. Here the guy on his hands is doing push-ups, and the orange things in the background are rocks that they use kettle-bell wise.

The culinary culture is to buy fresh things every day. So the streets are lined with stalls selling fruit and veg...Not all as beautiful as this!

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1971-2.jpg' | relative_url }})

Then we had a typical street food breakfast. Apparently this is the way it is done here, and in every street there are little stalls selling the chappatis and curries and a sweet number...

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1973.jpg' | relative_url }})
![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1976-2.jpg' | relative_url }})

The chappati was so light, the curries delicious and the sweet thing ...sweet...all for around $1.

Kuldeep, the guide, is an entrepreneur, and has several irons in the fire as well as tour guiding. In the summer here, when the temperature is in the 40s, when it rains incessantly and the Ganges floods, there aren’t many tourists. He has also a splendid organic, caftan and sandals sort of café called the Sparrow Cafe. I went there for lunch and it was excellent.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1987-2.jpg' | relative_url }})
![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1988-1.jpg' | relative_url }})

I had my first barber shave here...a splendid job, with head and shoulder massage included, for about $1. And I thought the shaves in Turkey were cheap!

The manager at this Airbnb (Purple Lotus Homestay and Learning Centre) is a jovial Indian named Santosh. He has been splendid in helping me have a good experience here. When I showed slight incomprehension as he gave me directions to the barber, he offered to take me on his motorbike. So off we went, me sans helmet, through the crazy traffic. Later he invited me to come with him and another friend of his to a post funeral eat-fest...

When someone dies here, they are cremated, and 13 days later there is a big feast for all the relatives and friends, and relatives of friends and friends of relatives. This one was under a marquee in the back terrace of the dead lady’s family house. Tables were set up, and the food distribution, by chaps dollopping the curries and rice from stainless steel buckets, and frizbeeing chappatis, commenced...at 4pm! It was all going strong at 8 when we arrived, and at 9.15 when we left. People sat and ate and chatted and stood around in groups, tables were cleared and the waiters (ie the ones who were waiting to eat) were signalled by the waiters.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1993-6.jpg' | relative_url }})

Here’s Santos and the other guy and the food. The food was excellent, and I managed quite well eating with my fingers...and only once or twice finding myself using the left hand. Noone else had his or her left hands anywhere near the food. The plates are made of pressed leaves...very little plastic here.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1994-1.jpg' | relative_url }})

We went there by motorbike...one motorbike. Three of us on the one bike, hurtling through the frantic traffic, dodging potholes, changing lanes at random, bipping the horn, for 45 minutes. Santosh tells me that there aren’t a lot of accidents.

There has been a festival to honour the goddess of learning and music, Saraswati. Marquees are set up all along the streets, housing decorated statues of the goddess, and accompanied by heavily amplified Bollywood style music. The effect is wonderful.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2020/01/img_1995-3.jpg' | relative_url }})

The idea of helicopter parents is absent, at least from those near the bottom of the socioeconomic rat race. This pair took my attention...
