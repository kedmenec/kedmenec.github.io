I am starting to write this on Sunday. It is a public holiday for National Sovereignty and Children's Day, referring to the opening of the first parliament of the Republic in 1920, and which Ataturk declared a day for children, they being the future of the new nation.

But that isn't a sufficient reason for my sitting in my hotel room in the middle of the day.

The other reason is that it's snowing outside, yep, snowing, and a well-digger's bum would be warm in comparison...we are halfway through Spring, and the 20+ degree days, with cloudless skies, and sunlight gushing over the tulips, have faded somewhat. By Tuesday, so the prognosticators tell us, we will be back to normal, but for now 4 or 5 degrees seems to be the best on offer. So, trudging through the streets on the lookout for points of interest has taken a back seat to the necessity for staying vaguely warm. The lightweight Turkish trousers and socks that I bought on the assumption that warmth was on the way are pitifully inadequate when faced with the icy blast.

I was driven by hunger to brave the streets at lunchtime, and I made it to a cafe around the corner, a place where I have lunched a few times before. The first time the waiters were polite and welcoming, but merely workmanlike, but the subsequent times they smiled, welcomed, shook hands (with me) and made me feel right at home. Each time I have skipped the meat-heavy first pages of the menu, and ended up with the mercimek çorbası, the delicious lentil soup that is available everywhere. This time I ordered a salad as well, and I was expecting the usual mixture of iceberg lettuce/spinach/rocket/parsley and cucumber, tomatoes, onions, olive oil and lemon juice. But no! The salad was composed of tomatoes, slices of raw garlic, and a green, fleshy plant that I had seen growing on the nature strip in Kerang, but hadn't encountered in a salad.

*(photo missing: tomato-300x225.jpg)*

I asked the waiter what it was called...semizotu came the reply. The 'ot' part means a weed, the final u is there for secret grammatical reasons, and a quick look in the dictionary revealed that 'semiz' means fleshy, porky, well fed. So the fat weed salad it was. And the dico also revealed that the English name is purslane. The natural food guru of Kerang, Anne Thompson, had told me of the great nutritional value of this plant, the plant we regularly chip out of the lawn and cast into the outer darkness, while chomping into the nutritionally vapid iceberg substitute. It tasted deliciously, all the more because I knew it was doing me good.

Tuesday  
I had another good experience in another restaurant. It was a bit up-marketer than the places I usually haunt, but despite the table cloths and waiters wearing shirts with collars, the prices were standard...5lira for soup (about $2) etc.

The first time I went, on the freezing day, and I togged up in everything warm, to eat there, the waiters were polite and we had a bit of hearty badinage when I asked what there was for vegetarians. There was a variety of prepared things in big dishes in a Bain Marie (it took me a while to work out what 'sulu' meant...they have it written on the posters outside these places, along with ızgara (grilled) or tava (done in a frying pan). The word means su=water and lu=with, so it could mean boiled. But it turns out to mean that the dishes of food are kept warm by the hot water in the Bain Marie. There is so much to learn!) and the smart arse cook said 'only soup, and this dish...chicken'. And we all laughed. It was just like 'my big fat Turkish wedding!' But I ended up with the soup and a salad, and they gave me a syrup drenched pudding delight as a gift, as well as as much tea as I could desire. And when I left it was handshakes all round. I went back today...

As soon as I hit the doorway there were hoş geldinizes at twenty paces, (that means welcome) and I felt like the prodigal son coming home. The cook, unprompted, rattled off the meat free options,  and I sat in the sun, preparing myself for more food than I was intending. The soup came, delicious, then the spinach and cheese pide. It was quite a meal, and the reception was food for the soul.

Turks have lots of honorifics, the titles they give you when no names are involved. With my friends here we rarely use names, but call each other 'dostum' which means my dear friend. In a shop or cafe, someone older gets called 'abi', which means 'older bro', and sometimes a suffix 'cim', which is pronounced 'jim', is put on the name. One of the waiters called me 'abicim' which means something like 'my dear little big brother'! One other friend calls me Terrycim sometimes, and I've heard little kids calling their fathers 'babacim'. We only have 'mate', or 'darl' from the checkout person. Hmm

Even though a week in a dusty big city might sound like a recipe for boredom, it is the experiences like the above which only happen when you are there for a while. The museums  and the mosques can be 'done' in a couple of days, but the memorable things take longer.

I 'did' a couple of museums today. There are the ruins of a castle, dating from Hittite times, 3000 odd BCE, on a big hill in the city, but it had been modified by every other conquering civilisation since then. Now only the shell remains with housing and streets in a very random and pleasing arrangement inside. I gather it was a rather slummy area until a decade or so ago, but big efforts have been made to glam it up, and splendid it is. The two story houses are at all angles, piled on top of each other, growing organically like crystals, and now whitewashed and sparkling.

*(photo missing: house-225x300.jpg)*

Around the castle were lots of merchant buildings and shops, some from 500 years ago, that have been carefully restored, and now house shops and a couple of museums. These contain the personal collections of stuff of significantly rich people who bequeathed the goods. One has all sorts of technological things...weapons, engines, cars, model ships and planes, books, clothing...and all presented magnificently. The other place has Greek vases and jewellery, coins, weapons, but just a few of each.

In the first museum I got talking to a middle aged guy who was in a workshop. He used to be an engineering technician, and when he retired, 10 years ago, was asked to be an exhibit in the museum, making old clocks, model steam engines and so on. He had a lathe and some other turning things and lots of tools, and he explained that it was his hobby, and he was lucky enough to score a workshop in a beautiful environment. He was most enthusiastic, and responded to my questions willingly. He showed me one engine that he had built, which involved heating the air in one cylinder containing a piston which forced the piston to move, then that air went to a cool cylinder which pulled another piston back. These were connected to a wheel, which was then caused to spin. He connected a rubber belt from that wheel to a dynamo, which then powered a light.

*(photo missing: man-225x300.jpg)*

He was a splendid fellow.  
He said that full sized engines of this type were used in Spain up until 10 years ago, for generating electricity, and that some had used mirrors or lenses to concentrate the sun's rays to do the heating.

The OH and S sensibilities here aren't overdeveloped to the degree that they are in the wide brown land. Footpaths regularly have gaping holes, uneven paving, sudden drops, and building sites seem like disasters waiting to happen. Blokes using jack-hammers in the streets don't wear ear or eye protection. I spied these guys doing scaffolding on the mosque outside my hotel window. Big boys' monkey bars here...

*(photo missing: builders-225x300.jpg)*

Gasp!

Tomorrow I'm off to Bursa, a few hours by bus to the west. And on Sunday back to Istanbul.

Enjoy the rain
