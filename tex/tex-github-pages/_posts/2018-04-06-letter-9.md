Greetings,

I had a pretty hefty dose of culture shock when I got back to Istanbul.

It was Saturday, beautiful day, and the crowds were out in force. The ride from the airport to the centre, on a train for a while then a tram for the rest, was a pretty cosy affair, with body to body contact fairly constant. My major concern, ensconced as I was in the heaving mass of warmish bodies in the region furthest from the doors, and holding onto my luggage like a man possessed, was...how was I going to get off the vehicle when my stop came up?

Without the bags it would have been relatively easy to insinuate the self through the interstices while muttering some universal platitude, but encumbered as I was, I feared for the worst...the worst what?...I don’t know, but fears make cowards of us all. So when I judged that the time was right I forged toward the door, and all went well: only a couple of small children terrorised and one old man even more convinced that all foreigners are the same.

People waiting to get on a tram, here, and I expect people almost everywhere, stand right in front of the train doors and impede the egress of those inside. In Singapore there are lines on the ground which effectively keep the getter-oners to the side while the getter-offers do their egressing, but Singapore is a special case. However, here, with both groups attempting some sort of mutually penetrating quantum simultaneous existence and nonexistence, the results are predictably chaotic.

In Izmir life is slower and less crowded.

The Turkish word for crowded is kalabalık, and it contains the word balık, which means fish. The tram ride here on Saturday had a striking similarity to a tin of sardines.

I caught up with another friend, yet another salesman that I’d had a good chat with last year, and this time spent an hour drinking tea in the sunshine and sharing stories. He and his family a refugees from Syria, from Aleppo. His father was an English teacher. The guy, Saleh now works in a shop selling silver things in a trendy street near the Blue Mosque.

On Tuesday I flew to Antalya on the south coast. It has been here for a couple of millennia, and the Roman bits from then are still evident...here’s the gate that the sycophantic locals built for a visit by the emperor Hadrian in the second century. No hastily constructed bash-up job here, this was built to last.

*(photo missing: F9D13B6C-3F38-4600-9371-F975F05C6090-1024x768.jpeg)*

Here’s a statue of Hadrian. I’m not at all taken with his hair do, but there’s no accounting for fashion.

*(photo missing: C969777F-6180-403A-A5D2-A923A291B2CD-e1522876441648-225x300.jpeg)*

There is an old town here, built around a harbour. The buildings have been splendidly restored, and the narrow winding streets now sport restaurants, crafty shops, carpet places etc. It’s an excellent place to stroll.

*(photo missing: 62575B84-2BFC-4D02-9EAF-1C7E683ADDD6-768x1024.jpeg)*

Not really winding, but pretty narrow.

The sea looks splendid, and there are parks and plazas all along waterfront.

*(photo missing: 38B77BF8-1E7D-4C23-B2E1-F9AB45167C8B-1024x768.jpeg)*

That’s snow on the mountain. It is seriously mountainous around here. The weather has been excellent...sunny, 23, slight cooling breeze, but a carpet guy I spent a while chatting with said that in August it is hot. The tourist season is just starting now, and there are some bright young things, and some rather faded others pounding the pavement already, but when it’s in full swing it’s a different matter. This place is very popular.

The museum is chock full of Roman statues, Greek vases, coins and stuff. There are some tombs as well...this one seemed to be sending a rather splendid vision of the afterlife ...or maybe it was a reminder of what the life before death could or should be.

*(photo missing: B65B0839-64F2-4D04-9F60-6362599FF256-1024x768.jpeg)*

Leaves the angels and harps a bit in the shade, but every day would be a bit of an effort.

I had dinner in one of the restaurants near the hotel on Tuesday night. The tables were outside on the cobbled street, there were lights here and there, the evening sky was velvet, a slight breeze fanned the warm air, and snatches of songs floated by. And the food was great.

![]({{ '/tex/assets/uploads/2018/04/A9EFE80B-F49A-45A1-A7FC-FA8A56AF5BCA.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Not a lot of others eating, but the season is just starting and there are a lot of restaurants.

On Thursday night I ate there again, and was treated as a longtime customer, this time given a plate of olives at the start, and a plate of fruit at the end to buttress the fish and salad that I was to pay for in the middle.

On Wednesday night I had a drink with a friend from 12 years ago. His name is Uğur Kaya, and he was working in a towel shop in a village near Pamukkale, a tourist and historical site west of here. We had kept chatting on messenger a bit over the years. He’s now a manager at a swish hotel here, and very good to chat with. His wife was with us too; she’s a Pilates instructor and great to be with.

*(photo missing: A487EF3D-7593-49C2-A267-1345122B04E0-1024x897.jpeg)*

The old city  has lots of boutique hotels, many in renovated Ottoman period houses. My place is airy, bright, whitewashed, and has only a few rooms. It’s very comfortable and quiet, and the manager is very easy to talk with.

*(photo missing: 63559CDA-4DB5-43F1-A509-8C1112A0D36F-768x1024.jpeg)*

The breakfast was enough to last the whole day through...

*(photo missing: 5B5EF3C1-F25A-4801-AC6D-B310CD41AB23-1024x832.jpeg)*

The dish on the right held an egg.

On top of most apartment buildings here there are forests of solar hot water installations, and satellite TV dishes. I haven’t seen any solar power panels though.

Thursday’s excursion was a full day visiting some archaeological sites at nearby places Aspendos, Side and Perge. The main things to see were Roman period ruins, many in excellent condition. Here’s the amphitheatre at Aspendos. It could hold 15000 spectators...

*(photo missing: A07C6F9F-762F-4C6E-B603-954000839D5E-e1523241636162-300x225.jpeg)*

Today I’m going by bus a bit north to Isparta, which is the rose growing centre of Turkey. I don’t suppose there will be many roses out as it is a bit early.

I am enjoying this part of the trip greatly. It is so good to be able to mooch around, chatting to shopkeepers, waiters, anyone really.

Take care to be happy,  
T
